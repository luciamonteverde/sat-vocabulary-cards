Debacle

/dih-BAH-kul/ noun

Hear It
What It Means

Debacle means a sudden, humiliating failure or collapse

Show it in context

Debacle means a sudden, humiliating failure or collapse. The company's smart-speaker launch was a debacle: the demo units refused to answer a single question on stage, and the livestream cut out during the apology.

See How It Works · a worked example, then try one yourself
Worked Example

The televised awards ceremony turned into a debacle when the teleprompter died, the host announced two wrong winners in a row, and the network cut to commercials while producers argued on a live microphone.

A polished, high-profile event collapses suddenly into public embarrassment through one failure after another. That kind of sudden, humiliating breakdown of a planned event is a debacle.

Prefixde-un-, undo
Rootbaclebar, barrier (French bacler, 'to bar')

Debacle was borrowed whole from French debacler, 'to unbar or break loose' (de-, 'un-,' plus bacle, 'bar'), and originally described river ice violently breaking apart in spring; English kept that image of everything suddenly breaking loose at once. The French root does not build other common English words, so there is no root quiz for this one.

Use the root in a new word: derail

One hostile question managed to derail the entire press conference, sharing the same 'undo' prefix de- that starts debacle.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

Neutral / journalistic, often wry

Frequently used in news and commentary, typically as 'a complete debacle' or 'turned into a debacle,' with a tone of embarrassment. A debacle is a sudden, humiliating FAILURE of a plan or event; it is not about danger (that is 'perilous') or mere noisy confusion (that is 'commotion'). It is also stronger and more public than a simple 'setback.'
