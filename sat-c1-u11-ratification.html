Ratification

/rat-uh-fih-KAY-shun/ noun

Hear It
What It Means

Ratification means the act of formally approving an agreement, treaty, or set of rules so that it becomes official and legally binding.

Show it in context

Ratification means the act of formally approving an agreement or set of rules so that it becomes official and binding. The constitutional amendment sat unfinished for years until ratification by three-quarters of the states finally made it law.

See How It Works · a worked example, then try one yourself
Worked Example

The student council's new constitution was only a proposal until a schoolwide vote delivered its ratification in the final week of classes.

The document existed before the vote, but it had no official power. Ratification names the formal group approval that switched it from a draft into binding rules.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

Formal — legal and political

Use ratification for the official, usually voted, approval that makes a treaty, contract, constitution, or amendment legally binding — not for everyday agreement ('we ratified our lunch plans' would sound comically formal). The related verb is ratify. Ratification is the final step that turns a finished draft into a document with real force.
