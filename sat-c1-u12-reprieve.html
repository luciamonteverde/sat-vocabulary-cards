Reprieve

/rih-PREEV/ noun

Hear It
What It Means

Reprieve means an official postponement of a punishment or unwanted event — the trouble is delayed for a time, not canceled

Show it in context

Reprieve means an official postponement of a punishment or unwanted event — the trouble is delayed for a time, not canceled. The vendor won a reprieve when the hospital agreed to push back the contract's termination date while new terms were negotiated.

See How It Works · a worked example, then try one yourself
Worked Example

The century-old theater received a reprieve from demolition while the preservationists' paperwork made its way through the county office.

The wrecking crews have not been called off forever — the demolition is only postponed while the review happens. A delay with the threat still hanging in the air is exactly what a reprieve is.

Prefixre-back, again (Latin)
Root-prieveto take, seize — from Latin prehendere, arriving through Old French repris, 'taken back'

Re- ('back') plus a French descendant of Latin prehendere ('to take, seize'): a reprieve literally 'takes back' a prisoner from punishment for a while. The sentence waits — it does not disappear.

Use the root in a new word: comprehend

It took Jonah a moment to comprehend the map — the same 'seize' root that lets a reprieve take a prisoner back lets your mind take hold of an idea.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

Formal, with common figurative use

A reprieve is strictly temporary — it postpones a punishment or dreaded event but never erases it, so don't use it where you mean a pardon or a cancellation. It also thrives figuratively in everyday writing: a cool morning can be 'a brief reprieve from the heat wave.' Don't confuse it with reprise, a repeated section of music.
