Inimical

/ih-NIM-ih-kul/ adjective

Hear It
What It Means

Inimical means hostile, harmful, or unfavorable in nature or effect, especially when describing conditions or surroundings.

Show it in context

Inimical means hostile, harmful, or unfavorable in nature or effect, especially when describing conditions or surroundings. The thin, dry air of the high desert proved inimical to the ferns, which shriveled within days of being planted there.

See How It Works · a worked example, then try one yourself
Worked Example

A grower planted a row of young lemon trees a hundred yards from a windy shoreline. Salt spray coated the leaves at every high tide, and the sandy soil drained away every drop of fresh water within minutes. Nothing at the site was obviously poisonous, yet not one tree ever put out healthy growth. The conditions were simply inimical to citrus.

The site's conditions worked actively against the trees rather than merely failing to help them, which is what inimical describes.

Inimical comes from Latin inimicus, from in- ('not') plus amicus ('friend'), finished off with the suffix -al ('characteristic of, pertaining to'). Read the pieces in order and the word means 'characteristic of being un-friendly,' which is why conditions inimical to a crop behave like an enemy of it.

Use the root in a new word: amity

Two nations that sign a treaty of amity are pledging friendship, built on the very Latin amicus that inimical turns inside out.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

formal, academic and scientific register

Inimical is nearly always followed by 'to' plus whatever is being harmed, as in 'inimical to plant growth' or 'inimical to open debate.' It usually describes conditions, climates, policies, or environments rather than a person's temperament; for an unfriendly person, plain 'hostile' is the better word.
