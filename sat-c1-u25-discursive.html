Discursive

/dis-KUR-siv/ adjective

Hear It
What It Means

Discursive means rambling from topic to topic; lacking a clear, direct structure.

Show it in context

Discursive means rambling from topic to topic; lacking a clear, direct structure. The lecture was so discursive that students' notes wandered from beekeeping to tax law before ever touching the syllabus.

See How It Works · a worked example, then try one yourself
Worked Example

The best man's toast began with how the couple met. Four minutes in, it had detoured through his own college roommate troubles, a theory about parallel parking, and a story involving a borrowed kayak that never reached its point. Guests kept lifting their glasses hopefully, only to lower them again. The toast was warm, even funny in places — but so discursive that when he finally said 'to the happy couple,' half the room had forgotten whose wedding it was.

The toast's detours through roommates, parking theories, and kayaks — with no through-line back to the couple — show the topic-to-topic rambling that discursive names.

Build the Word · tap each piece to reveal its meaning
Prefixdis-apart; in different directions
Rootcursto run (Latin currere, cursus)
Suffix-ivetending to; having the nature of

Discursive decomposes perfectly: dis- ('apart, in different directions') + curs ('to run' — the root of cursor and courier) + -ive ('tending to'). Latin discurrere meant 'to run about in different directions,' so discursive speech is literally speech 'characterized by running off every which way.' The picture is built right into the morphemes: a discursive talker's thoughts are runners scattering from the starting line in different directions.

Use the root in a new word: courier

A courier literally 'runs' packages across town — currere, 'to run,' the same running root that lets a discursive speaker run off in every direction at once.
