Obstinate

/OB-stih-nit/ adjective

Hear It
What It Means

Obstinate means not yielding easily to argument or persuasion; stubborn.

Show it in context

Obstinate means not yielding easily to argument or persuasion; stubborn. The obstinate goat would not budge from the garden path, whatever the farmer tried.

See How It Works · a worked example, then try one yourself
Worked Example

The debate team had lost three rounds in a row using Lena's opening strategy. Her teammates showed her the judges' scoresheets and proposed a different order of arguments. The coach walked her through exactly where the strategy was leaking points. Lena crossed her arms and announced that the team would open the same way in the final round, too. Win or lose, she was too obstinate to change course.

Lena refuses to change her chosen course of action despite clear evidence and repeated arguments from everyone around her — that refusal to yield is what makes her obstinate.

Prefixob-against; in the way
Rootstinto stand (Latin stare)
Suffix-ateadjective-forming ending

Obstinate comes from Latin obstinare, 'to stand firmly against': ob- (against) plus a form of stare, 'to stand.' An obstinate person is literally one who takes a stand and will not be moved off it. The same standing root runs through stationary (standing still), statue (a standing figure), and steadfast.

Use the root in a new word: steadfast

Her steadfast support never wavered through the losing season — steadfast, like obstinate, is built on the idea of standing firmly in place, though it wears the idea as a compliment.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

neutral-to-disapproving; everyday and formal use

Obstinate is the general-purpose member of this unit's 'stubborn' trio: a broad refusal to change one's mind or course of action. Obdurate is harsher and more literary, implying a heart hardened against pity itself, while adamant points at emphatic, vocal insistence on one specific point.
