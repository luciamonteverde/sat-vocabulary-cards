Feasible

/FEE-zuh-bul/ adjective

Hear It
What It Means

Feasible means capable of being done or carried out; practically possible.

Show it in context

Feasible means capable of being done or carried out; practically possible. Finishing the treehouse in one weekend was feasible only because the lumber had already been cut to size.

See How It Works · a worked example, then try one yourself
Worked Example

The student council wanted to host a spring carnival. Before approving it, the principal asked one question: was the plan feasible? The treasurer priced the tents and games against the club's savings, the custodian confirmed the field would be free that weekend, and enough volunteers signed up to cover every booth. With the budget, the space, and the workers all accounted for, the council showed the carnival could realistically be carried out.

The council proved the carnival could actually be done with the money, space, and volunteers available, which is exactly what feasible means.

Suffix-ibleable to be

Feasible entered English from Old French faisable, 'doable,' built ultimately on Latin facere, 'to do or make,' the same root behind factory and manufacture. A feasible plan is, quite literally, one that can be done.

Use the root in a new word: facilitate

A good checklist can facilitate even a complicated move, literally making it easier to do, from the same Latin facere that built feasible.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

neutral; planning, business, and engineering register

Feasible describes plans, projects, budgets, and timelines, things to be carried out, and asks whether they can realistically be done with the time, money, and resources available. Related but distinct: plausible judges whether a claim or explanation seems believable, while feasible judges whether a plan can actually be executed; a plausible business idea may still not be feasible on a student budget.
