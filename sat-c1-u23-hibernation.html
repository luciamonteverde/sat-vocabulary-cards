Hibernation

/hy-bur-NAY-shun/ noun

Hear It
What It Means

Hibernation means a dormant, inactive state some animals enter to survive winter, marked by lowered body temperature and slowed metabolism.

Show it in context

Hibernation means a dormant, inactive state some animals enter to survive winter, marked by lowered body temperature and slowed metabolism. The dormouse spends more than half the year in hibernation, curled in a ball of grass beneath the hedge roots.

See How It Works · a worked example, then try one yourself
Worked Example

Researchers wired a hedgehog's nest box in a Devon garden and watched it through one winter. From November to March the animal's heartbeat fell from about 190 beats a minute to fewer than 20, and its body cooled to within a few degrees of the air inside the box. It ate nothing for nineteen weeks and lost a third of its body weight. Only when the soil warmed in late March did the hedgehog rouse and leave to hunt, ending its hibernation.

A heartbeat falling from 190 to under 20, a body cooled almost to air temperature, and nineteen weeks without food make up exactly the winter-long dormancy that hibernation names.

Roothibern-winter, wintry (Latin hiems, hibernus)
Suffix-ationthe process or state of

Latin hiems meant 'winter,' and its adjective hibernus, 'wintry,' produced the verb hibernare, 'to spend the winter.' English kept the whole idea in hibernation, 'the state of wintering over.' The rarer adjective hibernal still means simply 'of the winter,' and Roman soldiers' winter quarters were called hiberna from the same root.

Use the root in a new word: hibernal

Botanists call the bare winter form of a plant its hibernal state, from the same Latin hiems ('winter') that stands behind hibernation.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

scientific; also used loosely in everyday speech

In biology hibernation means a season-long, physiologically regulated winter dormancy, not simply a long sleep. Its close cousin torpor describes the same lowered-metabolism state on a far shorter clock: a hummingbird enters torpor for a single cold night, while a marmot's hibernation runs for months.
