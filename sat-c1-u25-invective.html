Invective

/in-VEK-tiv/ noun

Hear It
What It Means

Invective means a sharp, angry verbal attack; harsh, insulting language.

Show it in context

Invective means a sharp, angry verbal attack; harsh, insulting language. The coach was fined after unleashing a stream of invective at the officials' table.

See How It Works · a worked example, then try one yourself
Worked Example

The reservoir hearing opened with charts and careful questions. But when the vote went against the marina project, its lead investor seized the microphone. He called the commissioners frauds, sneered at the town engineer's degree, and questioned the honesty of everyone who had testified. Security finally cut the sound as the stream of invective showed no sign of slowing.

Calling the commissioners frauds and insulting everyone who testified is a sustained burst of harsh, insulting language — that verbal attack is what invective names.

Prefixin-against
Rootvectto carry (Latin vehere, vectus)
Suffix-ivetending to; having the nature of

Invective assembles neatly: in- ('against') + vect ('to carry', from vehere, the root of vehicle and convection) + -ive. Latin invectivus described speech 'carried against' someone — an attack delivered in words. One twist to notice: although -ive normally builds adjectives, invective has settled into English as a noun naming the attacking language itself, a reminder that -ive words occasionally drift into noun territory.

Use the root in a new word: convection

Warm air rises and cool air sinks in convection currents — vect, 'to carry,' the same root that makes invective an attack 'carried against' its target.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

formal, literary noun; disapproving of the speech it names

Note the part of speech: invective is a noun for a stream of insulting language, an act of speech. Keep it apart from this unit's pejorative, which describes a single disparaging term or label, and vindictive, which describes a revenge-hungry person, not language at all.
