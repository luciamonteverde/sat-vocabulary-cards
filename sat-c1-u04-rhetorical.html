Rhetorical

/rih-TOR-ih-kuhl/ adjective

Hear It
What It Means

Rhetorical means relating to the art of persuasive speaking or writing; (of a question) asked for effect, not expecting an answer

Worked Example

When Mr. Okada asked the class, 'Do we really want to be the group that gives up before the experiment even starts?', the question was rhetorical—he was already handing out the lab goggles.

No answer is expected; the question exists purely for effect, to rally the class. That is the classic rhetorical question. The word can also describe anything relating to the craft of persuasive speaking or writing itself.

Rootrhetor-from Greek rhetor 'public speaker, orator,' from the verb eirein 'to speak'
Suffix-ic'having the character of,' from Greek -ikos
Suffix-al'relating to,' from Latin -alis

Rhetor- (a public speaker) + -ic + -al means 'relating to the speaker's art'—the craft of using language to move an audience, which also gave us the special sense of a question asked purely for effect.

Use the root in a new word: rhetorician

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

academic; everyday in the fixed phrase 'rhetorical question'

In essays and criticism, rhetorical describes the craft of persuasion—rhetorical devices, rhetorical strategies. In everyday speech it lives almost entirely in the phrase 'rhetorical question,' one asked for effect with no answer expected. Note that calling something rhetorical says nothing about whether it actually convinces anyone—that would be persuasive.
