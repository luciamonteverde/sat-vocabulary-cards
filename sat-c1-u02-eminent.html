Eminent

/EM-uh-nunt/ · adjective

Hear It
What It Means

Eminent means widely recognized as outstanding, distinguished, and highly respected within a particular field.

Show it in context

The conference had drawn the most eminent geologists in the world, scholars whose names filled the very textbooks the students had studied all year.

See How It Works · follow the thinking, then try one

Here's how to decide when eminent is the exact word.

Worked Example

The eminent judge's landmark rulings on privacy law are still dissected line by line in first-year classrooms at every law school in the country.

Notice why eminent is the right word here: the judge is not merely known, she is widely recognized as OUTSTANDING and DISTINGUISHED WITHIN HER FIELD, and that recognition was EARNED through the quality of her legal work. That is the heart of eminent: prominence built on genuine expertise and the respect of one's peers. Do not confuse it with the look-alike imminent, which sounds similar but means ABOUT TO HAPPEN (an imminent storm, an imminent deadline) and has nothing to do with reputation. Also keep eminent distinct from a looser word like famous: a reality-TV star can be famous for pure spectacle, but eminent reserves its praise for people whose standing rests on ACHIEVEMENT AND MASTERY, not just visibility.

Which watchmaker is genuinely EMINENT?

Synonyms · words that mean the same — tap one to see how it differs
distinguished

The closest everyday match: marked out as excellent by a record of achievement. Slightly plainer than eminent, which carries a stronger sense of standing at the very top of a field.

renowned

Emphasizes the spread of one's fame — being widely talked about and known by name. Eminent leans more on the quality and authority behind that reputation than on how far the name has traveled.

esteemed

Highlights the respect and high regard others hold for the person. It foregrounds others' admiration, whereas eminent foregrounds the person's own demonstrated superiority.

Antonyms · opposites

obscure — An obscure figure is unknown and unnoticed, with no recognized standing in any field — the direct opposite of the earned, widely acknowledged distinction that eminent describes.

How Strong Is It?
competent→respected→eminent→legendary

This spectrum tracks degree of recognition within a field. A competent professional simply meets the standard; a respected one is admired by colleagues; an eminent one stands out as a distinguished authority whom the whole field recognizes; a legendary one has passed into near-mythic status, celebrated for generations. Eminent sits at step 3: clearly above ordinary respect, but grounded in real, living reputation rather than legend.

Build the Word

eminent = e- (out) + -minere (to project, jut out) → literally "to stand out." From Latin eminere, "to stand out, project, be conspicuous," formed from ex- "out" plus the root -minere "to project" (related to mons, "mountain").

The image built into eminent is physical: something that juts OUT above everything around it, the way a peak rises above a plain. An eminent person figuratively "stands out" above their peers in the same way.

A single prominent peak rose above the ridgeline, visible from every village in the valley below.

Which breakdown correctly shows how "eminent" is built from its Latin parts?

e- (out) + -minere (to project, stand out) → "to stand out"

✓ Correct — Correct. The e- means "out" and -minere means "to project," so eminent literally pictures someone standing out above the rest.

e- (not) + -minent (visible) → "not visible"

✗ Not quite — Not quite. This reverses the meaning. The e- here is a form of ex- ("out"), not a negative prefix, and -minere means "to project," so the word means standing OUT, not being hidden.

em- (in) + -inent (powerful) → "powerful within"

✗ Not quite — Not quite. The real prefix is e-/ex- meaning "out," not "in," and the root -minere means "to project." The idea is standing out, not being powerful inside.

The root connection →

prominent shares the very same root: pro- ("forward") + -minere ("to project") = "to project forward, jut out." So a prominent peak and an eminent judge are both, at root, things that STICK OUT above their surroundings. One more relative is worth noting carefully: imminent also contains -minere, but with im- ("upon, over") it means something "projecting over" you — hanging overhead, about to happen. Same root, different prefix, completely different meaning — which is exactly why the two words are so easy to mix up.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

formal / respectful

Eminent Eminent is a formal word of praise. Reserve it for people of serious, established standing in a profession or field — it would sound oddly grand applied to a friend or to someone merely popular.
