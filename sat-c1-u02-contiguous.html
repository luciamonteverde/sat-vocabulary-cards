Contiguous

/kun-TIG-yoo-us/ · adjective

Hear It
What It Means

Contiguous means sharing a common border or touching along an edge, with no gap in between.

Show it in context

Their two farms were contiguous, separated by nothing more than a low stone wall that both families had mended for generations.

See How It Works · follow the thinking, then try one

Here's how to decide when contiguous is the exact word.

Worked Example

The hotel gave us two contiguous rooms joined by a connecting door, so the kids could reach our room without ever stepping into the hallway.

Contiguous describes things that SHARE A COMMON BOUNDARY AND TOUCH WITH NO GAP IN BETWEEN. These two rooms are not merely close, or on the same floor — they meet along a shared wall, and the connecting door proves they are directly joined. Compare this with rooms that are only 'nearby': those might sit several doors apart, with other rooms wedged between them. The defining test for contiguous is DIRECT CONTACT — if you can fit anything at all between the two things, even a hallway or one empty room, they are close but NOT contiguous.

Synonyms · words that mean the same — tap one to see how it differs
adjacent

Means next to or very near, but does not strictly require touching — adjacent seats or lots can be side by side with a small gap or aisle between them. It is slightly looser than contiguous, which insists on actual contact.

adjoining

Emphasizes a shared common wall or boundary that physically connects two spaces, like adjoining offices. It is the closest match to contiguous, but it usually implies a single shared partition rather than any edge-to-edge meeting.

abutting

A more technical, often legal or architectural term meaning meeting end-to-end or edge-to-edge at a boundary. It stresses the precise line of physical contact, as when one property abuts another along a lot line.

Antonyms · opposites

detached — Detached means standing physically apart, separated by a gap or open space — the exact opposite of touching along a shared edge. A detached house sits on its own with clear space on every side, sharing no wall with its neighbors.

How Strong Is It?
distant→nearby→contiguous→fused

This spectrum tracks increasing closeness and connection. 'Distant' things are far apart with much space between them; 'nearby' things are close but still separated by a gap; 'contiguous' things actually touch along a shared border yet remain distinct units; and 'fused' things have merged into a single whole with no boundary left at all. Contiguous sits at step 3 — in direct contact, but not yet melded into one.

Build the Word

con- (together) + tangere (to touch) From Latin contiguus, 'touching, adjoining,' built from con- 'together, with' and the verb tangere 'to touch.' The same tangere root gives English tangent, tangible, tactile, and contact.

Literally 'touching together.' The con- signals that two things come into contact, and tangere pins down the exact kind of contact — touch. So a contiguous boundary is one where two things touch each other with nothing between them.

In geometry, a tangent is a straight line that touches a circle at exactly one point without crossing into it.

Which breakdown captures the literal meaning of 'contiguous'?

con- (together) + tangere (to touch) = 'touching together'

✓ Correct — Correct. Con- means 'together' and tangere means 'to touch,' so contiguous literally describes things that touch one another — exactly the sense of sharing a border with no gap.

contra- (against) + -guous (to guide) = 'guiding against'

✗ Not quite — Not quite. The prefix is con- ('together'), not contra- ('against'), and there is no root meaning 'to guide' here. The true root is tangere, 'to touch.'

con- (together) + tenere (to hold) = 'holding together'

✗ Not quite — Not quite. 'Con- + tenere (to hold)' gives us words like contain, not contiguous. The root in contiguous is tangere, 'to touch,' which is why it means things touching along a border.

The root connection →

Both contiguous and tangent grow from Latin tangere, 'to touch.' A tangent line touches a curve at a single point without cutting through it; contiguous regions touch each other all along a shared border without any gap. Once you see tangere = 'touch,' the family clicks together: contact is a 'touching-with,' something tangible can be touched, and contiguous things are the ones physically in touch.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

Neutral to formal.

Contiguous Contiguous is a precise, somewhat formal word common in geography, law, real estate, and even computing (as in 'contiguous memory'). Reach for it when you specifically mean 'touching with no gap'; in casual speech, 'next to' or 'side by side' usually sounds more natural.
