Capacious

/kuh-PAY-shus/ — rhymes with spacious · adjective

Hear It
What It Means

Capacious means roomy enough to hold or contain a very great deal, with plenty of open space inside.

Show it in context

The old theater's capacious lobby swallowed the entire crowd without ever feeling full, its marble floor stretching well past the last row of coat racks.

See How It Works · follow the thinking, then try one

Here's how to decide when capacious is the exact word.

Worked Example

The walk-in closet was so capacious that it held her entire wardrobe, three suitcases, and a full-length mirror, with room left over to stand and dress comfortably in the middle.

Capacious means having a LARGE INTERIOR SPACE ABLE TO HOLD A GREAT DEAL. It's not just big on the outside (a solid boulder can be huge yet hold nothing); it's specifically about how much OPEN ROOM there is INSIDE to contain things. The closet swallowing a whole wardrobe plus suitcases and still leaving space to move is exactly what capacious describes.

Your Turn

A sculptor unveiled an enormous granite statue in the town square, a solid block of carved stone that towered three stories above every rooftop nearby.

Is capacious the precise word for this statue, or should it just say 'massive'?

Choose the word that fits — then tap it to check.

massive

✓ Correct — The statue is a solid block of stone: it is huge, but it has no interior space to hold anything. 'Massive' captures its great size without wrongly implying it can contain things.

capacious

✗ Not quite — Capacious specifically means roomy inside, able to hold a great deal. A solid statue holds nothing, so however big it is, capacious doesn't fit; 'massive' is the accurate word.

Synonyms · words that mean the same — tap one to see how it differs
spacious

Spacious means having plenty of open space. Capacious is more specific: it stresses the great amount something can hold or contain, not just an open, airy feel.

roomy

Roomy is the casual, everyday word for comfortably ample space. Capacious is stronger and more formal, emphasizing sheer holding capacity rather than mere comfort.

commodious

Commodious means roomy and comfortable or convenient to use. Capacious focuses purely on how much can fit inside, saying nothing about comfort or convenience.

Antonyms · opposites

cramped — Cramped means uncomfortably small, with too little room to hold or move. It is the direct opposite of capacious, which describes generous interior space that holds a great deal.

Build the Word

cap- Latin: 'to hold, contain'

Capacious comes from the Latin capax, built on capere, 'to take, hold, contain' — capacious things have a great deal of room inside to hold or contain.

The stadium was filled to capacity, with every one of its ninety thousand seats taken well before kickoff.

Using the same root, what does capacity most literally refer to here?

the amount that something can hold or contain

✓ Correct — Capacity comes straight from capere, 'to hold, contain,' so it names the maximum amount something can hold, just as a capacious space is one that holds a great deal.

the speed at which something can move

✗ Not quite — Speed has nothing to do with capere ('to hold, contain'). Capacity is about how much can be held, not how fast.

the height that something can reach

✗ Not quite — Height isn't part of the root capere ('to hold, contain'). Capacity measures how much can be contained, not how tall or high something is.

The root connection →

The Latin root cap- / capere means 'to take, hold, contain.' It links capacious (able to hold much) to capacity (the amount that can be held), and to a wider family including capable, capture, and container. Once you see cap- as 'hold,' a capacious room and a filled-to-capacity stadium are clearly two sides of the same idea: how much space there is to contain things.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

formal / literary

Capacious is a formal, somewhat literary word, at home in descriptive writing about buildings, vehicles, containers, and even the mind ('a capacious memory'). In everyday speech, say 'roomy' or 'big inside,' but reach for capacious when you want to emphasize that something holds an impressively great deal.
