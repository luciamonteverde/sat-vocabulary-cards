Stratification

strat-uh-fih-KAY-shun noun

Hear It
What It Means

Stratification means the arrangement of people or things into distinct, ranked layers or levels.

Show it in context

Beneath the polite dinner conversation lay a rigid stratification, in which the seating itself announced who stood above whom.

See How It Works · a worked example, then try one yourself
Worked Example

On the naval vessel, the crew was divided into sharply ranked layers, with senior officers berthed on the upper decks, petty officers just below them, and enlisted sailors packed into the cramped lower quarters near the hull.

Stratification means arrangement into DISTINCT, RANKED LAYERS stacked one above another by status or level. It is not just any grouping or sorting (you can separate people into equal categories that have no top and no bottom); stratification specifically requires LAYERS THAT SIT ABOVE AND BELOW ONE ANOTHER in a fixed order of rank. The crew split across higher and lower decks strictly by rank is the definition of stratification.

Rootstrat-something spread or laid down; a layer (Latin stratum, from sternere, 'to spread out')
Suffix-ficationthe act or process of making (from Latin facere, 'to make, do')

Latin sternere meant 'to spread out,' and its past participle stratum named 'something spread or laid down' — in other words, a layer. Add -fication, built on facere ('to make'), and you get 'the act of making into layers.' Stratification is literally layer-making: taking a whole population, society, or material and laying it down in distinct sheets, one stacked above another — which is why the word always implies ranked levels, never just separate side-by-side groups.

Use the root in a new word: substrate

Chemists prepared a clean glass substrate first, since every thin film they deposited afterward had to bond to that base layer underneath.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

Formal / academic

Stratification is the precise term for arrangement into ranked layers, especially in sociology, geology, and ecology. In everyday speech, say 'the class system,' 'levels,' or 'pecking order,' but stratification is the exact word when you want to emphasize a whole system divided into distinct, ranked layers.
