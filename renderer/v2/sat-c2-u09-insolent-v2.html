insolent

/IN-suh-luhnt/ - the stress falls on IN

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Insolent means rude and arrogantly disrespectful.

See the word in context

The traffic judge had heard every excuse, but the driver's insolent slow clap as the fine was read out was something new.

When the museum guard asked to see his ticket, the teenager held it an inch from her nose, let it flutter to the floor, and strolled on into the gallery. She had the authority; he wanted her to know exactly how little it weighed with him. That performance — rudeness with a sneer of superiority riding on top — is insolent.

Insolent is the exact word because the rudeness points upward and wears arrogance: he isn't just impolite, he is showing a person in authority that her authority means nothing to him. Cheeky would be a wink with no harm meant; insolence means the contempt is the message.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    impertinent: An impertinent remark oversteps the speaker's place — a liberty the room has not granted, taken out of nerve or simple carelessness. Insolent rudeness goes further and means it: aimed up at authority on purpose, arrogant, with the contempt itself as the message.

    disrespectful: Disrespectful is the umbrella word for rudeness toward someone owed better, in any tone from sulky to loud. Insolent is its sharpest corner: disrespect aimed up at authority and delivered with arrogance, as if the authority itself were the joke.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    respectful: gives authority its due

    courteous: polite in form and manner

    Where it sits · how far the defiance has hardened

    cheeky → insolent → mutinous

    cheeky vs. insolent: A cheeky answer grins and dances at the line; an insolent one walks over the line and dares you to mention it.

    mutinous vs. insolent: Mutinous goes past insolent: the insolent sailor mocks the order while obeying it, but the mutinous one is done obeying — disrespect ripened into revolt.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    sharp / judging

    Insolent is disrespect aimed up the ladder — a student to an examiner, a clerk to an inspector — delivered with arrogance rather than anger, as if the authority itself were the joke. Its unit-mates map the other directions: imperious is arrogance looking down, presumptuous a line quietly overstepped. If the tone is fond and the room laughs along, you've only got cheek.

    The school inspector's visit had gone smoothly until he reached the back row of the sixth form. Asked to name the principal export of the region, Vikram Rao stood, bowed with theatrical depth, and delivered a flawless answer in a pitch-perfect imitation of the inspector's own drawl. The class froze. The imitation was not affectionate, and Vikram did not pretend it was: he held the man's eye through the whole performance and bowed again at the end. The headmaster's report called the display insolent, and chose the word with care — the boy's answer had been correct in every particular, and that was part of the offense. The rudeness was not error or nerves; it was aimed, and everyone in the room, the inspector included, understood exactly where.
