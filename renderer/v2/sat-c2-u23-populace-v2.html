populace

/POP-yuh-lus/

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

The populace is the ordinary people of a place — the general mass of its residents, as opposed to its elite.

See the word in context

When grain ran short, the emperor worried less about the senate than about the populace of the capital.

The new tax fell hardest on the populace — the dockworkers, shop clerks, and tenant farmers who could least absorb it — while the merchant princes who had drafted it paid almost nothing. Within a year, the city's ordinary thousands had made the law unenforceable.

The populace is the broad mass of ordinary people in a place, seen from above and counted in thousands: everyone outside the privileged few. Rulers, planners, and pamphleteers speak of the populace when they mean the general run of residents rather than any particular person.

    Accuracy

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    public: The public is people in their role as audience or customers — you address the public, poll the public, serve the public. The populace is people as the mass of a place's residents: you count it, feed it, govern it.

    citizenry: The citizenry is a place's people seen as holders of civic rights — voters and jurors. The populace is broader and plainer: everyone who lives there, voter or not.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    elite: the small, privileged circle at the top

    aristocracy: the hereditary noble class above the common people

    Where it sits · how many people the word sweeps in

    crowd → populace → humanity

    crowd vs. populace: A crowd is a much smaller and briefer thing than a populace — it lasts an afternoon and fills one square, while a populace takes in everyone who lives in the place, gathered or not.

    humanity vs. populace: Humanity goes further than a populace: a populace stops at the borders of one city, valley, or nation, while humanity counts every person alive everywhere.

      Section 3

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      neutral / civic — the census and history-book word

      Populace names the governed many, and writers reach for it when the view is from above — proclamations, censuses, sieges. Two neighbors to keep straight: populous is an adjective meaning full of people (a populous city), and a populist is a politician who claims to champion the populace. The people, the crowded, and the champion — three different words.

      In 1854, the harbor city of Veynes hired a young engineer to modernize its waterworks. The council imagined new fountains for the promenade, where its own villas stood. The engineer began instead with a count. Ledger by ledger, she tallied the populace of the city: dockworkers, seamstresses, lodgers, children — every resident, whether or not their names appeared on any voting roll. Her report was blunt. Nine in ten residents drew water from four failing wells, while the promenade already enjoyed three fountains. The council protested that visitors judged a city by its promenade. She answered that cholera did not tour the promenade; it moved through the lanes where most residents actually lived. The new mains went to the lanes first. Within a decade the wells were closed, and the fever season that had once emptied the schools each summer never returned.
