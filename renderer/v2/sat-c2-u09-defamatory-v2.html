Meaning

Hear It

What It Means

Defamatory means harming someone's reputation with false statements.

See the word in context

The historian sued over the documentary's defamatory claim that he had invented his interview tapes.

Mid-season, a nameless fan site posted that the goalkeeper had bet against her own club. No bookmaker, regulator, or teammate ever backed the story, but the transfer offer she had been weighing vanished within the week. When the club's lawyers called the post defamatory, they meant something precise: a published falsehood that had cost her the one thing a career runs on — her name.

Defamatory is the exact word because it needs both parts: the claim must be false, and it must wound a reputation. A true exposé can end a career and defame no one; an insult — 'a mediocre keeper' — bruises feelings but states an opinion. The defamatory statement pretends to be fact, gets the fact wrong, and leaves the damage standing.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    slanderous: Slanderous is defamatory harm in spoken form — the false accusation said aloud, at a meeting or over a phone line. Defamatory is the umbrella term for a reputation-wrecking falsehood in any medium; slanderous commits to speech and speech alone.

    libelous: Libelous is defamatory harm in fixed form — print, posts, broadcast — the falsehood you can file away and read back. Where defamatory covers a reputation-damaging lie however it travels, libelous commits to the written or recorded version of it.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    flattering: presenting someone more favorably than the facts strictly allow

    complimentary: openly expressing praise

    Where it sits · how far past honesty the attack goes

    unflattering → insulting → defamatory

    unflattering vs. defamatory: An unflattering profile is far milder than a defamatory one: it shows your bad side and stays within its rights, because every word of it may be true.

    insulting vs. defamatory: An insulting remark is offensive to your face and may still be honest; defamatory goes further — false, public, and aimed at what the whole world thinks of you.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    formal / legal

    Defamatory is the courtroom's word: it accuses a statement of being false and of damaging someone's standing, and both halves must hold. Its unit-mate scurrilous judges style, not truth — a scurrilous attack is coarse and abusive whether or not a word of it is false, while a defamatory one can be phrased as politely as a legal brief.
