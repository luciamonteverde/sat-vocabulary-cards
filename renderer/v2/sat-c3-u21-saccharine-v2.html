Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Saccharine means sweet or sentimental to a sickening degree.

See the word in context

The pop station's saccharine holiday playlist — all sleigh bells and key changes — sent half the office reaching for headphones by December third.

The retirement banquet's slideshow turned saccharine fast: soft-focus baby photos, a ballad on loop, and a rhyming toast that compared the retiree to a sunrise, a lighthouse, and a dove.

Saccharine is sweetness accused of being fake: saccharin, the chemical it points to, is a lab-made sugar substitute, and the adjective keeps that aftertaste — feeling that has been sweetened artificially, to formula, rather than felt.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    sugary: Sugary keeps to the surface — a sugary frosting, a sugary singing voice — sweetness you can taste or hear, with no verdict on the feeling behind it. Saccharine passes that verdict: the sweetness is fake, worked up to formula, and pushed until it sickens.

    schmaltzy: Schmaltzy is showbiz-sentimental — emotion laid on thick in performance, the crooner's catch in the voice — and it can be said with a grin. Saccharine is the harsher charge: the feeling itself is fake, oversweetened to a sickening degree. A schmaltzy act can still delight.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    tart: sharp-edged and refreshing rather than sweet

    understated: kept deliberately quiet and unsweetened

    Where it sits · how little real feeling remains

    tender → sappy → saccharine

    tender vs. saccharine: Tender stops short of saccharine — tenderness is real feeling, gently expressed, with no sugar added.

    sappy vs. saccharine: Sappy stops short of saccharine — sappy oversweetens honest feeling, while saccharine substitutes syrup for the feeling altogether.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    critical / dismissive

    Saccharine and cloying — both in this unit — fail in different directions: cloying is real sweetness in fatal quantity, the fourth spoonful; saccharine is sweetness of the wrong kind, synthetic and unearned, the greeting-card rhyme. A dessert is usually cloying; a speech is usually saccharine.

    When the Maple Street Diner hired an ad agency, the owner, Rosa, asked only that the commercial feel like the place. What came back was thirty seconds of syrup: golden light, a grandmother who had never worked there, and a slow piano under the slogan 'Where every cup is poured with love.' Regulars winced. One posted a review calling the ad saccharine — sweetness so fake, he wrote, that it made his teeth ache and his stomach turn. Rosa agreed. The diner's charm was burnt coffee, crowded booths, and waitresses who teased you; nothing about it was gentle or golden. She pulled the spot and replaced it with unedited footage of the morning rush. Business, and the regulars' mood, recovered within the week.
