armistice

/AR-mih-stiss/

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

An armistice is a formal agreement between warring sides to stop fighting.

See the word in context

The armistice signed in a railway carriage at Compiègne silenced the Western Front at eleven o'clock on November 11, 1918.

The Korean War ended with an armistice, not a peace treaty: in 1953 the commanders signed a halt to the fighting, drew a buffer zone between the armies, and went home. Because nothing more was ever signed, the two Koreas remain technically at war to this day.

An armistice stops the fighting without ending the war. It is formal — negotiated, signed, binding on every front — which lifts it above a local truce; but it settles nothing about borders, blame, or terms, which is why the peace talks come after.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    truce: A truce is any agreed pause in the fighting — it can be local, informal, even unspoken, and as short as an afternoon. An armistice is the formal version: negotiated, signed, and binding across the whole war.

    ceasefire: A ceasefire orders the guns quiet, often in one sector and often as a first, fragile step. An armistice goes wider and deeper — a signed agreement between the warring commands that halts the war everywhere at once.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    hostilities: open fighting between the two sides

    escalation: a deliberate widening or sharpening of the conflict

    Where it sits · how far the halt in the fighting reaches

    ceasefire → armistice → peace

    ceasefire vs. armistice: A ceasefire is weaker than an armistice: it quiets the guns for a stretch or a sector, while an armistice binds every front under one signed, formal halt.

    peace vs. armistice: Peace goes further than an armistice — an armistice only stops the shooting while the war legally continues; peace ends the war itself, with borders drawn and treaties signed.

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      formal / historical

      Armistice is the historian's word for a war formally stopped but not yet settled — Armistice Day took its name from one. Keep the ladder straight: a ceasefire quiets a sector, an armistice halts the whole war on paper, and peace ends it. Its unit-mate détente is different again: rivals easing tension, no shooting war required.

      In the museum's hall of documents, one yellowed page draws the longest lines: the armistice that ended the fighting between the two republics after eleven years of border war. The generals of both armies signed a single document halting combat on every front, and couriers carried the news to the farthest outposts within a day. Guides are careful to explain what the page did not do. The war would not legally end for another two years, when a treaty finally drew the new border. Veterans interviewed for the exhibit describe the strange quiet of that first week, when the guns stopped everywhere at once but no one yet dared call it victory. For them the signature meant one thing only: the shooting was over, even if the argument was not.
