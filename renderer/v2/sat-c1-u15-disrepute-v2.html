disrepute

/dis-ri-PYOOT/

Meaning

What It Means

Disrepute means a state of being held in low regard or having a bad reputation.

See the word in context

Years of falsified data brought the once-celebrated research laboratory into disrepute, and other scientists quietly stopped citing its studies.

Sunny Terrace, once the most popular restaurant on the boardwalk, fell into disrepute after inspectors published a string of serious kitchen violations. Within a month, its dining room sat nearly empty even on summer weekends.

The restaurant's damaged reputation, and the public's loss of trust in it, shows exactly what it means to fall into disrepute.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    disgrace: Disgrace is the sharp public fall itself - the scandal breaks and someone is disgraced, often in a single humiliating stroke. Disrepute is the lasting condition that follows: the settled state of being held in low regard. A minister resigns in disgrace; the ministry he leaves behind sits in disrepute.

    dishonor: Dishonor is shame that stains a person's own honor - a soldier's dishonor, a broken oath - and it measures the fall against a personal code. Disrepute is outward-facing and impersonal: the low standing a business, practice, or theory sinks into when other people stop trusting it.

    shame: Shame is the painful feeling of having lost standing - it burns from the inside, whether or not anyone else ever finds out. Disrepute is entirely external: other people's low regard, the damaged reputation itself. You can feel shame in total privacy; disrepute exists only in the public eye.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    esteem: high respect and admiration

    prestige: widespread respect based on reputation

    Where it sits · how badly a name is stained

    unpopularity → disrepute → infamy

    unpopularity vs. disrepute: Unpopularity just means people do not like something at the moment, while disrepute means its reputation itself has been seriously damaged.

    infamy vs. disrepute: Disrepute means being held in low regard, but infamy means being widely famous for something truly shameful.

      formal

      Disrepute almost always appears in the fixed phrases 'fall into disrepute' and 'bring something into disrepute.' It names the lasting state of a damaged reputation, not a single embarrassing moment.

      The Grand Meridian was once the hotel in Havenport -- senators in the ballroom, honeymooners on the roof garden, a waiting list three months deep. Its slide into disrepute took less than a decade: cut corners on cleaning, unanswered complaints, a kitchen twice cited by inspectors. No single scandal brought it down. Guests simply stopped trusting the name, and travel writers began adding a warning where the stars used to be. The current owner insists the building is sound and the staff loyal, and both claims may be true. But reputation, a rival manager observed, is the one fixture a hotel cannot reorder from a catalog once the public has marked it down.
