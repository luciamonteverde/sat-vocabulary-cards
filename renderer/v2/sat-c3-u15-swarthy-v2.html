What It Means

Swarthy means having a dark complexion.

See the word in context

The opera's costume notes call for a swarthy pirate king, his dark complexion set off by a scarlet coat.

In the memoir, the author's swarthy uncle stands out in every photograph of the pale northern village — olive-skinned and dark-haired, the one face the neighbors never mistook for anyone else's.

Swarthy is a literary, slightly old-fashioned word for having a dark complexion. It describes coloring only — most often in ballads, novels, and old records — and says nothing about character; modern writing outside such contexts usually just says 'dark-skinned' or 'tanned.'

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    dusky: Dusky is a poet's word for soft, dark tones — dusky light, a dusky glow, a dusky complexion — describing shade in the moment rather than its source. Swarthy is strictly a complexion word: naturally dark skin, named in an older literary register. Dusk can fall on anything; swarthy fits only people.

    dark-complexioned: Dark-complexioned is the plain, modern way to say someone has naturally dark coloring — the wording a passport office or a news report would choose. Swarthy means the same thing in an older, literary voice, at home in ballads and sea novels. The difference is register, not shade.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    fair: light in coloring

    pale: lacking color, sometimes from illness

    Where it sits · how dark and lasting the coloring is

    fair → tanned → swarthy

    fair vs. swarthy: Fair names the lightest coloring on this scale, as far from swarthy as a complexion can be — the pale end of the ladder.

    tanned vs. swarthy: Tanned is darkness the sun lends and winter takes back; it stops short of swarthy, which names a complexion that is naturally deep-toned.
