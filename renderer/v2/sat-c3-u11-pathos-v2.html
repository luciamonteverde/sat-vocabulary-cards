Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Pathos means a quality that stirs pity or sympathy.

See the word in context

The shelter's fundraising letter gets its pathos from one line: the terrier still waits by the gate at the hour his family used to come.

Directors of war films know the pathos lives in small props, not battle scenes: the unopened letter in a coat pocket, the boots by the door that no one will wear again. Audiences sit dry-eyed through explosions and break at a pair of boots.

Pathos names the quality in the work that stirs pity — not the sadness the audience feels, but the power in the scene that draws it out. It is a critic's word, and in classical rhetoric it names the appeal to emotion itself.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    pity: Pity is the ache the audience feels — sorrow stirred by someone else's suffering, the lump in the throat as the lights come up. Pathos sits on the other side of the footlights: not the feeling itself but the quality in the scene that draws that feeling out of a room.

    poignancy: Poignancy is the everyday word for a moment's piercing, bittersweet sting — one photograph, one pause that catches you off guard. Pathos is the critic's term for the same power, and it is broader: in classical rhetoric, pathos names the whole appeal to an audience's emotion.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    bathos: the pratfall version — a plunge from moving to accidentally ridiculous

    hilarity: open laughter, the opposite effect on a room

    Where it sits · how strongly the audience is moved

    sentiment → pathos → heartbreak

    sentiment vs. pathos: Sentiment is milder than pathos — a touch of tender feeling, while pathos genuinely stirs an audience to pity.

    heartbreak vs. pathos: Heartbreak goes further than pathos — the audience is no longer just moved to pity but genuinely devastated.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    critical / rhetorical

    Pathos is the analyst's word: critics and rhetoricians use it for the quality in a work or speech that stirs pity — in classical rhetoric, the appeal to emotion. The audience feels the pity; the scene has the pathos. Its unit-mate poignant does the everyday version of this job: a poignant scene is one a critic would say is full of pathos.

    Our drama club's spring production was a courtroom play, and for weeks it ran flat. The acting was fine and the staging clean, yet the audience stayed dry-eyed. Ms. Vega, the director, said the script had logic but no pathos — nothing in the scenes themselves reaching out to squeeze the room. So she rebuilt one moment. The defendant's daughter, silent all play, now folds her father's coat over the rail as the verdict is read. Nobody on stage cries. Nobody asks the audience for anything. But on opening night you could hear the rows go quiet, and by the second show, ushers were leaving tissue boxes by the door. The scene had not changed its facts — only its power to move.
