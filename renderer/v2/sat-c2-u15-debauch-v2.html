debauch

/dih-BAWCH/ - the stress falls on BAWCH

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Debauch means to corrupt someone morally by leading them into indulgence.

See the word in context

Tabloids accused the film set of debauching its youngest star, who arrived eager and left unable to keep a call time.

The boy there on a full ride joined the supper club in October. By March his tutors barely saw him: the club's older members had debauched him by degrees — keeping him out till dawn, paying his tabs, laughing at his deadlines, and treating every warning from the dean as a joke. His grades did not ruin him; his new appetites did.

Debauch is the exact word for what the club did, because it means to corrupt someone morally by leading them into indulgence — the damage is done with pleasures, not threats. Corrupt covers bribes and rigged books; debauch names the slower ruin that arrives dressed as hospitality.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    deprave: To deprave someone is to make them morally bad through and through — the finished corruption, however it was reached. Debauch names one particular route there: pleasure and indulgence laid on until appetite does the damage that force never could.

    lead astray: To lead someone astray is the gentle, roomy phrase — the friend led astray may only have skipped homework or missed a curfew. Debauch is the far end of that same road: morals dissolved outright in pleasure and excess.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    reform: turn someone back toward decent habits

    edify: build character up rather than dissolve it

    Where it sits · how far the character gets dragged down

    tempt → corrupt → debauch

    tempt vs. debauch: Tempt is far milder than debauch — a temptation is a single dangled lure, one dessert or one shortcut, and it can still be refused; debauching someone takes a steady diet of indulgence.

    corrupt vs. debauch: Corrupt goes further than tempt — the character itself starts to bend; debauch claims the most: a character dissolved wholesale in pleasure, ruin made into a habit.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    formal / literary, faintly antique

    Debauch belongs to court histories and cautionary tales — fortunes, wasted heirs — and it always works through pleasure. Its neighbors divide the labor: corrupt is the plain broad verb and takes bribes as readily as banquets, while this unit's debase lowers a thing's worth rather than a person's morals. If the ruin arrived by envelope, say corrupt; if it arrived by invitation, debauch.

    Historians of the late empire loved a moral: the frontier legions stayed hard, and the capital's garrison went soft. The city, one chronicler wrote, could debauch a soldier faster than any enemy could kill him. The process was never dramatic. It was wine with breakfast, then races in the afternoon, then a paid substitute standing his watch. No single luxury did the damage. Each one could have been refused; the steady diet of them, year over year, was another matter. By the time the frontier veterans marched through the gates, the chronicler claimed, the garrison could no longer form a straight line.
