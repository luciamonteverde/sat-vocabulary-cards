protectionism

/pruh-TEK-shuh-niz-uhm/ - the stress falls on TEK

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Protectionism means the policy of shielding a country's own industries from foreign competition, as with import taxes.

See the word in context

The island's protectionism begins at the dairy case: a stiff duty doubles the price of every imported cheese so the local creamery can match it.

In the 1950s the republic wrapped its infant car industry in protectionism: foreign sedans paid half their value at the docks, buyers waited years for the homely domestic model — and the factory, shielded from every rival, saw no reason to improve.

Protectionism is a policy word and often an accusation: it bundles duties, quotas, and licensing walls into a single doctrine of shelter. Its defenders say jobs; its critics say cosseted industries and pricier goods — note how the -ism turns a practice into a program.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    nativism: Nativism favors the native-born over immigrants — a politics about people. Protectionism favors home industry over foreign goods — a politics about products. The two often ride the same platform, but one guards the makeup of the workforce while the other guards the market for what it makes.

    isolationism: Isolationism is the broader turning-inward: a country pulling back from alliances, treaties, and foreign engagement across the board. Protectionism walls off commerce only — duties and quotas on goods — and a protectionist nation can still sign defense pacts and attend every summit.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    free trade: borders open to goods, with no duties in the way

    openness: the general welcome protectionism walls off

    Where it sits · how far outside trade is shut out

    favoritism → protectionism → autarky

    favoritism vs. protectionism: Favoritism is the milder lean — a thumb on the scale for local firms here and there; protectionism builds the lean into law, with duties at every port.

    autarky vs. protectionism: Autarky goes further than protectionism: protectionism taxes what crosses the border, while autarky tries to need nothing from across it at all.

    When cheap imported shoes began filling the shelves, the island's two remaining shoe factories asked the assembly for help. The help arrived as a border tax: every foreign pair is charged forty percent at the port, before it ever reaches a store. Supporters call the policy simple fairness -- local workshops keep their workers, and the money collected funds the port besides. Critics answer that protectionism always sends the bill to shoppers, who now pay more whether they buy foreign shoes or local ones. Both sides agree on what the policy is: a wall of charges built to shelter home industry from foreign competition.
