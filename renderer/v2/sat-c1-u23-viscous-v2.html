viscous

/VIS-kus/

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Viscous means thick, sticky, and slow-flowing rather than free-flowing.

See the word in context

The molasses had gone so viscous in the cold pantry that it climbed the spoon in a single rope instead of pouring.

A woodworking shop keeps two jars of linseed oil, one by the stove and one out on the unheated porch. Poured from the warm jar, the oil spreads across a board in a few seconds. Poured from the cold jar, the same oil takes almost a full minute to cross the same board, and the stream from the neck stretches without breaking. Apprentices are told to warm the porch jar before varnishing, because oil that viscous leaves ridges no brush can smooth out.

It is the same oil in both jars, yet the cold jar's oil takes a minute to travel where the warm oil took seconds and stretches in an unbroken rope, and that slow, thick, resistant flow is what viscous names.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    thick: Thick is the everyday word for a liquid that resists pouring, and it stretches to fog, hair, and crowds besides. Viscous is the precise term for one property only: a fluid's internal resistance to flow, the kind a lab can measure and compare.

    gooey: Gooey is the playground word for soft, sticky stuff -- melted marshmallow, mud between the toes -- and it is about how things feel to the touch. Viscous is the measured word for how a fluid flows, at home in a lab report rather than a kitchen.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    runny: thin and flowing freely

    watery: as thin and quick-flowing as water

    Where it sits · how much it resists flowing

    syrupy → viscous → gluey

    syrupy vs. viscous: Syrupy is the friendly kitchen version of viscous, describing a thickness you can still pour without much trouble.

    gluey vs. viscous: Gluey is stronger than viscous, adding the idea of sticking fast to a surface rather than merely flowing slowly across it.

      Section 3

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      descriptive and semi-technical

      Viscous describes a liquid that still flows, only slowly and thickly; once something stops flowing altogether it is no longer viscous but tacky, gummy, or simply solid. Watch the spelling trap too, since viscous (thick) has no connection at all to vicious (cruel).

      Crude oil does not always gush. From the tar-heavy fields of the far north it emerges so viscous that engineers describe it as reluctant — a fluid that would rather stay put. Pipelines are designed around this reluctance. Pumping stations sit closer together on heavy-oil lines, each one shoving the slow column forward another stretch. Operators also borrow a trick from the kitchen: heat. Warm oil flows more willingly, so some lines are wrapped in insulation and gently heated for hundreds of miles. Another approach thins the cargo itself. Blenders stir in a light solvent until the mixture loosens enough to travel at ordinary pressure. Every choice is a bargain with the same property: how stubbornly the fluid resists flowing, and what it will cost to overcome that resistance.
