What It Means

Coronation means the formal ceremony in which a new king or queen is invested with royal authority.

See the word in context

Crowds pressed against the palace gates for the coronation, waiting hours to glimpse the new queen as she was handed the scepter and orb.

The network anchors kept reminding viewers that, however sleek and modern the broadcast looked, the coronation itself was the single moment their new king legally assumed royal authority.

A coronation is not just a party, a broadcast, or a grand public occasion — it is the formal ceremony in which a new monarch is invested with royal authority. Looser words like "celebration" or "ceremony" tell you only that something festive or ritual is taking place; they do not specify that a king or queen is being granted the legal right to rule. The concerts, parades, and street parties around the event were celebrations, and the televised pageantry was a ceremony — but the coronation was the one act that actually transferred sovereign power. Reach for "coronation" only when the point is the official investiture of a monarch, not merely the festivities surrounding it.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    crowning: Crowning names the physical act itself — the moment the crown is set on the monarch's head. Coronation means the entire formal ceremony built around that gesture: the oaths, the anointing, the regalia, and the whole legal and religious rite.

    investiture: An investiture formally confers any office or authority along with its insignia — a knight, a bishop, or a chancellor can each receive one. A coronation is narrower: it is specifically the investiture of a monarch, centered on the crown.

    installation: Installation is the general term for formally placing anyone in an office or post — a judge, a dean, a board chair. A coronation is one specific, royal kind of installation: the rite that crowns a monarch and grants full royal authority.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    abdication: The formal act of giving up royal authority and stepping down from the throne — a coronation begins a reign; an abdication ends it.

    dethronement: The removal of a monarch from the throne, often by force — where a coronation installs a ruler, dethronement strips one of power.

    Where it sits · how completely royal power is granted

    accession proclamation → enthronement → coronation

    accession proclamation vs. coronation: An accession proclamation merely announces that a new sovereign has succeeded — a herald's declaration, with none of the sacred ritual a coronation carries.

    enthronement vs. coronation: An enthronement formally seats the ruler on the throne, but a coronation goes further, crowning the monarch with the full regalia and investing complete royal authority.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    formal, dignified, and historical

    "coronation" is a formal noun tied to monarchy, history, and journalism, so it fits ceremonial, news, or scholarly writing. Keep it for the literal crowning of a king or queen. Using it loosely for any big promotion or triumph — "his coronation as CEO" — is figurative and can sound grandiose or lightly ironic, so choose that extended sense deliberately.

    Medieval kings often waited months to hold a coronation, and the delay was strategy, not laziness. A dead king's heir ruled at once in law. But ruling and being seen to rule were different things, and the great ceremony existed to close that gap. Every element was staged for witnesses: nobles swearing loyalty aloud, holy oil on the new king's brow, the regalia carried through the crowd piece by piece. Chronicles rarely dwell on the moment the crown touched the head. They describe the oaths, the anointing, and who stood where -- the machinery of consent. A wise heir therefore waited until rivals could attend and be seen kneeling. The rite mattered most as a public act witnessed by the whole realm.
