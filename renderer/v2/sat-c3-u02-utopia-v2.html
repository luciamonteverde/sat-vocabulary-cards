Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Utopia means an imagined place of perfect arrangement.

See the word in context

To its ten-year-olds the summer camp is a utopia: no clocks, no grades, and dessert twice a day.

For the 1893 World's Fair, Chicago raised a gleaming 'White City' — electric streetlights before most homes had bulbs, spotless boulevards, its own police force and water plant. Guidebooks sold it as a utopia made real, a preview of the perfect city. It was staff-managed scenery, of course: behind the plaster facades, the perfect city had no residents at all.

Utopia is not just any wonderful place — it means an imagined place of perfect arrangement, a society engineered so well that courts, shortages, and complaints wither away. Paradise delights the senses; utopia satisfies the planner. The word's own coiner built the catch in: in Greek it means 'no-place.'

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    idyll: An idyll is a small pocket of blissful simplicity — a honeymoon month, a cabin summer — found rather than designed. A utopia is a whole society engineered into perfection on purpose, every need anticipated in the plan. An idyll happens to a few people for a while; a utopia is drafted for everyone, forever.

    dreamland: Dreamland is an imagined happy place with a childlike ring — soft, hazy, and vague about the details. A utopia is imagined too, but it satisfies the planner: institutions worked out, provisions specified, nothing left to wishing. Dreamland floats on a wish; a utopia comes with a blueprint.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    dystopia: an imagined place where everything has gone wrong

    nightmare: a situation as dreadful as a bad dream

    Where it sits · how much of life it sets right

    haven → paradise → utopia

    haven vs. utopia: A haven is merely safe — shelter from one particular storm — far short of a utopia.

    paradise vs. utopia: Paradise goes further than a haven, delighting in every direction, but utopia goes further still: a whole society arranged so perfectly that nothing needs fixing.

      Section 3

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      literary / often skeptical

      Utopia carries its coiner's wink — 'no-place' — so writers reach for it half in longing, half in doubt: a planned society too perfect to exist. Paradise is delight without a blueprint; a haven is merely safe. The adjective utopian usually leans critical: a utopian scheme is one the speaker suspects will not survive contact with people.

      In 1843 a printer named Silas Hale sold his shop, bought prairie land, and drafted what he plainly called a utopia. His notebooks specified everything: the width of the streets, the rotation of crops, the hours of schooling, even the menu for common suppers. On paper, no member of the colony could ever be poor, idle, or quarrelsome, because the design left those things no room. The colony itself lasted nineteen months, undone by a dispute over who washed the supper dishes. Historians keep his notebooks anyway, less as a plan that failed than as a perfect record of what one century believed a perfect society would need.
