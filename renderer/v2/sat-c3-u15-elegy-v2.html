Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Elegy means a mournful poem, especially one lamenting the dead.

See the word in context

The anthology closes with an elegy the composer later set to music: eight stanzas grieving a violinist who died young.

When the newspaper's last printing press was hauled away, the retiring editor wrote an elegy in verse for the final edition — mourning the clatter of the machines and the night crews who ran them — and readers clipped it like an obituary.

An elegy is defined by its mourning, not just its seriousness: odes praise and ballads tell stories, but an elegy grieves — classically for a person who has died, and by extension for anything truly lost.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    eulogy: A eulogy is the speech given at a funeral or memorial, praising and remembering the dead aloud. An elegy is a mourning poem, written to be read. The mix-up is the classic test trap: the eulogy is spoken at the service, the elegy grieves in verse.

    dirge: A dirge is a slow, heavy song of mourning, made to be sung or played — often while a coffin is carried. An elegy grieves in written verse; the dirge lives in music and procession, the elegy on the page.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    ode: a poem of celebration and praise

    toast: brief spoken words in honor of someone living

    Where it sits · how formally the grief is shaped

    lament → elegy → requiem

    lament vs. elegy: A lament can be as raw as a cry at the graveside — grief before it finds a form; it stops short of an elegy, which shapes that grief into finished verse.

    requiem vs. elegy: A requiem goes further than an elegy in ceremony: where the elegy is a poem one voice can read, the requiem sets mourning for choir and orchestra inside a full rite.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    literary

    Elegy lives in classrooms and book reviews: use it for a poem (or something poem-like — a song, a film) whose business is mourning. If the piece praises without grieving, it is an ode; if it is the speech delivered at the funeral itself, that is a eulogy — the classic mix-up worth avoiding on test day.

    When the mine closed and took the town's last hundred jobs with it, the librarian did not write a history. She wrote an elegy. It ran thirty-one stanzas, one for each year the shaft had operated, and it named no villains. It grieved instead: for the whistle nobody would hear again, for the payroll Fridays, for the men and the two women of the last crew. She read it aloud only once, at the library, then printed copies for anyone who wanted one. People framed it. Decades later, visitors find it hanging in kitchens all over the county, still doing its quiet work of mourning.
