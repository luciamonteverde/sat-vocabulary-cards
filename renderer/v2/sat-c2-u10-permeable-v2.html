Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Permeable means allowing liquids or gases to pass through its own substance.

See the word in context

The new plaza is paved with permeable brick, so a cloudburst drains down into the soil below instead of sheeting across the crosswalks.

Two rain jackets can look identical on the rack and behave like different species on the trail. The cheap shell is a plastic bag with sleeves: sweat has nowhere to go, and by the second mile the hiker is wet from the inside. The expensive one is engineered to be selectively permeable — its membrane's openings are too small for rain droplets to enter but large enough for water vapor to escape, so moisture passes out through the fabric itself.

Permeable describes a material that substances can pass through — not around, not over, but through the stuff itself. Chemists rate membranes by it, farmers judge soils by it, and the word travels well into metaphor: a permeable border, a permeable line between work and home. Whatever crosses, the emphasis stays the same — the barrier admits passage.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    porous: Porous says the material is riddled with tiny holes — a report on its texture. Permeable reports the result instead — things get through — however they manage it: a membrane with no visible pores at all can still be permeable to vapor.

    absorbent: Absorbent material drinks liquid in and holds it — a bath sponge doing its job. Permeable material is a corridor rather than a cup: what enters keeps going and comes out the other side.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    impervious: allowing nothing to pass through or penetrate

    watertight: sealed so that no liquid gets in or out

    Where it sits · how freely things pass through

    watertight → permeable → porous

    watertight vs. permeable: Watertight is the weaker rung on this ladder of letting things through: a watertight seal admits nothing at all, while permeable material admits at least some of what presses against it.

    porous vs. permeable: Porous goes further than permeable: a permeable membrane may pass water vapor slowly and selectively, but a porous material is so riddled with openings that almost anything soaks straight through.

      Word Choice

      technical / neutral

      Permeable is lab and field vocabulary — soils, membranes, pavements — and it stays exact in metaphor: a permeable border is one that things actually cross. Two boundaries to hold. The adjective belongs to the barrier, never to whatever travels through it; and it is not absorbent, which drinks liquid in and holds it rather than letting it pass along.

The potters mix fine clay with sawdust; in the kiln the sawdust burns away, leaving channels finer than a hair running through the walls. The finished pot looks solid, but it is permeable: water seeps through the walls and drips into a clean bucket below, while most microbes are left behind in the clay. A batch is tested by timing it. If a full pot passes about two liters an hour, the walls are letting water through at the right rate. If nothing drips after an hour, the walls fired too dense, and the pot is rejected -- it can hold soup, but it cannot filter anything.
