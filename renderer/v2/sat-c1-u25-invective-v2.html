invective

/in-VEK-tiv/

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Invective means a sharp, angry verbal attack; harsh, insulting language.

See the word in context

The coach was fined after unleashing a stream of invective at the officials' table.

The reservoir hearing opened with charts and careful questions. But when the vote went against the marina project, its lead investor seized the microphone. He called the commissioners frauds, sneered at the town engineer's degree, and questioned the honesty of everyone who had testified. Security finally cut the sound as the stream of invective showed no sign of slowing.

Calling the commissioners frauds and insulting everyone who testified is a sustained burst of harsh, insulting language — that verbal attack is what invective names.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    abuse: Abuse is the broadest word for insulting, degrading language, in any register — one muttered slur already counts. Invective is abuse at full rhetorical flood: a sustained, often loud and elaborate stream of insults delivered as an attack. A single ugly name is abuse; ten unbroken minutes of it is invective.

    denunciation: Denunciation means fierce public condemnation — it formally accuses its target of wrongdoing and calls on others to judge. Invective needs no charge at all: it is the harsh, insulting language itself, attacking the person rather than making a case against them.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    praise: words of approval

    tribute: a statement of admiration and respect

    Where it sits · how sustained and bitter the abuse is

    insult → invective → vitriol

    insult vs. invective: An insult is weaker than invective: an insult is a single cutting remark, while invective is a sustained stream of them.

    vitriol vs. invective: Vitriol is stronger than invective — vitriol suggests language so corrosively bitter it burns like acid, while invective can be loud and abusive without that concentrated cruelty.

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      formal, literary noun; disapproving of the speech it names

      Note the part of speech: invective is a noun for a stream of insulting language, an act of speech. Keep it apart from this unit's pejorative, which describes a single disparaging term or label, and vindictive, which describes a revenge-hungry person, not language at all.

      Eighteenth-century London printers grew rich on political quarrels, and none paid better than the pamphlet war of 1768. What began as a dispute over a rigged election spent exactly one page on the evidence. After that, both sides abandoned argument for invective. Week after week, fresh pamphlets poured out comparing the rival candidates to swamp rats, pickpockets, and plague — sustained, inventive name-calling with no charge left in it, only venom. Readers stopped following the case entirely; they bought each new pamphlet the way one buys tickets to a fight.
