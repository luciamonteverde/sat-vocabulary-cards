Meaning

Hear It

What It Means

Antipathy means a strong dislike, repugnance.

See the word in context

A lifelong antipathy toward cities kept the poet on her mountain, where the mail truck's engine was the loudest thing she ever heard.

The conductor's antipathy toward amplified instruments is forty seasons old: not one microphone has ever stood on his stage. It is not fear — he mixes recordings happily in the studio — and not a mood of the moment. It is a settled, reasoned dislike, argued in two books and a hundred interviews, and it has outlasted every trend that was supposed to soften it.

Antipathy is the exact word because it names a strong dislike — one with roots. Mere distaste would fade or bend; his has structure and stamina. Note what antipathy does not require: he needn't flee amplifiers in terror or cut their cables. The feeling is deep, steady opposition — dislike as a fixed star.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    disdain: Disdain is dislike from above — it looks down on its object as beneath serious consideration, with contempt built into the posture. Antipathy meets its object at eye level: a strong, settled dislike that can still respect what it opposes. You can hold an antipathy for work you take seriously; disdain refuses to take it seriously at all.

    bitterness: Bitterness is the sour after-feeling of being wronged — it looks back at an injury and keeps tasting it. Antipathy needs no injury at all: the conductor was never harmed by a microphone; he simply holds a strong, settled dislike of what it does. Bitterness grows from a wound; antipathy can grow from pure judgment.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    fondness: warm liking

    affection: tender devotion

    Where it sits · how deep the ill feeling runs

    dislike → antipathy → loathing

    dislike vs. antipathy: Dislike is the pocket-sized version — real, but light enough to forget; antipathy has depth and a history.

    loathing vs. antipathy: Loathing goes further than antipathy: the dislike has curdled into something closer to hatred, with disgust in it.

      Word Choice

      formal / literary

      Antipathy is dislike with roots — settled, strong, usually aimed at a kind of person or thing. Where this unit's aversion shows itself in avoidance (you steer around the thing), antipathy can stand and argue. And unlike enmity, it needn't be returned: a critic can hold an antipathy the whole world overlooks.

      Chef Almeida owns every gadget her students bring her, keeps them oiled, and even demonstrates them fairly. Her antipathy toward the machines is a matter of judgment, not injury: none has ever ruined a dish of hers. She simply holds, and has held for thirty years, that a blade you cannot feel teaches a cook nothing. Visitors expecting contempt are surprised; she praises the engineering the way one praises a rival's good move. The dislike runs deep all the same -- deep enough that her own knives are the only tools she has ever taken to a competition.
