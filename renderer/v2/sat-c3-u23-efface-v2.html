What It Means

Efface means to wipe out or rub away.

See the word in context

Generations of pilgrims' fingertips have begun to efface the bronze lion's mane, polishing the curls into smooth bright metal.

The letters on the lab's oldest keyboard have been effaced by a million keystrokes — E, A and S are bare black squares now, and visitors type by memory or not at all.

Efface is more precise than remove or destroy because it means wearing a mark off a surface little by little — rubbing, friction, and use doing quietly what no single act did.

    Accuracy

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    erase: Erase is the everyday, deliberate act of removing a mark — one pass of the rubber and the pencil line is gone. Efface is slower and quieter: the mark is worn away little by little, by friction, weather, and use, with no single act doing the work.

    abrade: To abrade is to scrape a surface away by friction — strictly physical, and aimed at the surface losing material. Efface is aimed at the mark: wear removes the lettering, the image, the inscription. Sandpaper abrades wood; countless fingertips efface the letters painted on it.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    engrave: cut a mark in deep so that it lasts

    preserve: guard something from wear and loss

    Where it sits · how completely the mark disappears

    dim → fade → efface

    dim vs. efface: Dim is the gentlest change, far short of efface — a dimmed mark merely loses brightness and is still plainly there, while an effaced one has been worn off the surface entirely.

    fade vs. efface: Fade is stronger than dim but still stops short of efface: a faded mark survives as a ghost, while an effaced one is gone from the surface altogether.

      Section 3

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      literary / precise

      Efface is precise and a little literary: it names gradual removal from a surface — wear, weather, rubbing, grain by grain. Erase is the everyday deliberate act, and expunge, taught in this unit, is the official word for wiping an entry from a record rather than a mark from a surface. People can also efface themselves: self-effacing means keeping quietly to the background.

      Look up in any old mill town and you can still find them: 'ghost signs,' hand-painted advertisements fading high on the brick walls. A century of rain, frost, and sunlight has slowly effaced most of them. Each year the painted letters sit a little thinner on the brick, and some walls now show only a stain where a word used to be. No one scrubbed them off, and no crew was ever sent up on ladders. The weather did the work a little at a time, too gradually for anyone to notice it happening. Preservationists photograph the survivors every summer, comparing prints year over year to measure exactly how much lettering the seasons have taken. In another fifty years, they estimate, the brick will keep its color while the words painted across it disappear altogether.
