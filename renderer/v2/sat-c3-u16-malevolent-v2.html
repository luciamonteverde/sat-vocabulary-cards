malevolent

/muh-LEV-uh-luhnt/ - the stress falls on LEV

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Malevolent means wishing harm to others; showing ill will.

See the word in context

The novel's fog feels almost malevolent, curling under doors as if it meant the sleepers harm.

The troll king of the saga is not greedy but malevolent: he floods valleys he cannot rule, simply so that no one else may live in them.

Malevolent points at the will, not the act: a malevolent person wishes harm even while smiling, even before doing anything. It is stronger than spiteful (petty stings over slights) and colder than hostile — the harm is wanted for its own sake.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    venomous: Venomous is hatred that poisons what a person says and how they look at you — the malice is out in the open, dripping from words. Malevolent sits deeper and quieter: the settled wish for harm itself, which can stay perfectly polite while it waits.

    hostile: Hostile means openly antagonistic — an enemy who lets you see the opposition. Malevolent is colder and more hidden: it wishes real harm even while smiling, and it needs no declared fight. A hostile guard glares at you; a malevolent one quietly loosens the ladder.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    kindly: warm and well-meaning toward others

    warmhearted: generous and sympathetic by nature

    Where it sits · how dark the intentions run

    unfriendly → spiteful → malevolent

    unfriendly vs. malevolent: Unfriendly is a cold shoulder at most — nowhere near malevolent's settled wish for real harm.

    spiteful vs. malevolent: Spiteful wants to sting over a slight; it stops short of malevolent, which wishes lasting harm on principle.

      Word Choice

      formal / literary

      Malevolent describes a will bent toward harm — villains, curses, regimes. Spiteful is petty and personal; truculent (this unit) wants a fight in the open; malevolent can smile while it wishes you ill, which is what makes it the coldest of the three.

He was famous for his generosity: he analyzed openings with juniors, praised opponents in the press, carried spare sets to tournaments. It took her years to see the pattern. The lines he 'generously' recommended to rising players had flaws he had already worked out at home, traps he could spring months later across the board. Two careers, she writes, stalled exactly where his advice had pointed them. 'He never said a harsh word,' the chapter ends. 'He didn't need one. The harm was the hobby; the kindness was the tool.'
