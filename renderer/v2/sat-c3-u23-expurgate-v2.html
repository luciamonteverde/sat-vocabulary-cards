expurgate

/EKS-per-gayt/ - the stress falls on EKS

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Expurgate means to remove the offensive or objectionable parts of a text.

See the word in context

The anthology's editors expurgated the sailors' shanties, so the printed verses end two lines before the sailors did.

Before the school broadcast, the drama teacher expurgated the radio play, cutting the duel and the tavern brawl but keeping every turn of the plot intact.

Expurgate is narrower than edit or shorten because it targets only what might offend — the duel and the brawl went, not because the play was too long, but to make a clean version for young ears.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    censor: To censor is for an authority — a state, a school board, a platform — to suppress or forbid content outright, sometimes blocking the whole work. Expurgate is the editor's milder act: the offensive passages are cut, but a cleaned edition still reaches readers.

    bowdlerize: Bowdlerize means to expurgate prudishly and excessively, scrubbing anything remotely improper — the word mocks Thomas Bowdler's scrubbed Shakespeare. To expurgate is the neutral, professional version; bowdlerizing is the same cutting carried to a fussy, disapproving extreme.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    restore: put the removed passages back in place

    retain: keep the disputed passages where they stand

    Where it sits · how thoroughly the text is scrubbed clean

    soften → cut → expurgate

    soften vs. expurgate: Soften is far milder than expurgate — the rough joke stays in the text, just eased by a word or two, while expurgating strips the offending passages out entirely.

    cut vs. expurgate: Cut is stronger than soften but narrower than expurgate: you cut one scene, while expurgating means combing the whole work and stripping every part that could offend.

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    formal / editorial

    Expurgate is an editor's word, and a precise one: the cuts aim at offense, not length. Truncate, taught in this unit, shortens by lopping off an end; expunge, also in this unit, annihilates an entry from a record. Censor is the heavier cousin — an authority forbidding content — where expurgating produces a cleaned edition that still gets published.

    When the poet Ines Varga died in 1902, her sister Alba prepared the letters for publication — and prepared them thoroughly. Alba expurgated the collection before the printer ever saw it, cutting every feud with a rival, every barbed remark about a critic, and every line about money. The volume that appeared in 1904 was admired for its serenity; readers met a poet who seemed never to have quarreled with anyone. A century later, scholars located the originals in a bank vault and printed them uncut. Set side by side, the two editions tell different stories: one a monument, the other a life.
