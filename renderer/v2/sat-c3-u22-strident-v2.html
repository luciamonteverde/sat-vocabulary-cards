Hear It

What It Means

Strident means loud and harsh at once, with a scraping edge that is impossible to tune out.

See the word in context

The subway's brakes greeted every stop with a strident shriek that made tourists wince.

The school's ancient intercom made even the lunch bulletins strident — a raw, scraping blare that had teachers flinching mid-sentence — and the applause on the day it finally died was the loudest sound of the term.

Strident is sound with an abrasive edge — loud, harsh, and impossible to tune out. A voice, an alarm, or a tone of argument can all be strident; the word always carries disapproval, which separates it from merely loud, and its grating texture from shrill, which is only about pitch.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    shrill: Shrill is all about pitch — a sound piercingly high and hard on the ears, whatever its volume or texture. Strident is the fuller assault: loud, harsh, and grating at once, and always said with disapproval. A referee's whistle is shrill; a loudspeaker that is loud and rasping at once is strident.

    grating: Grating is about texture alone — a sound that rasps against the nerves like a file, even at low volume. Strident needs loudness as well: the harsh, loud, grating whole, disapproval included. A dry hinge merely grates; an old intercom at full cry is strident.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    dulcet: sweet and easy on the ear

    mellow: soft, warm, and rounded in tone

    Where it sits · how punishing the sound is

    loud → strident → ear-splitting

    loud vs. strident: Loud is milder than strident — volume alone, with no edge to it.

    ear-splitting vs. strident: Ear-splitting is louder and harsher than strident: strident scrapes at your patience, while ear-splitting simply overwhelms it.

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      neutral / disapproving

      Strident condemns a sound — or a tone of argument — as loud and grating at once. Shrill fixes on pitch, grating on texture, strident on the whole assault. It pairs naturally with voices, alarms, and rhetoric; nothing gentle is ever strident.

      For twenty years the ice-cream truck's jingle had been part of Maple Street's summers, and nobody had ever complained. Then the speaker began to fail. What had been a tinny but cheerful tune became strident: blared at full volume through a torn cone, every note arrived with a metallic rasp that carried three blocks. Residents who once waved now winced. A retired music teacher wrote to the county that the truck was 'an assault in waltz time,' and a petition gathered ninety signatures in a weekend. The driver, baffled, pointed out that the tune itself hadn't changed -- same notes, same tempo, same route. He was right, and it didn't matter. The problem was never the melody; it was the harsh, deafening delivery. A new speaker cost him eighty dollars and ended the war overnight.
