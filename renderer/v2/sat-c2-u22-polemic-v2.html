Meaning

What It Means

Polemic means an aggressive written or spoken argument against a specific position.

See the word in context

What the editor assigned was a balanced survey; what came back was a polemic, page after page prosecuting the case against zoos.

The food critic's notorious 1998 essay is a polemic against the fork — she wants diners to feel texture again — and for six pages she concedes nothing: no exception for soup, none for salad. Nobody has ever mistaken it for a review.

A polemic is an aggressive argument against a specific position, and the aggression is the point: it prosecutes rather than weighs. It differs from a rebuttal, which answers an argument someone actually made to you; a polemic charges at a standing target, invited or not.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    broadside: A broadside is a full-force verbal attack fired all at once — the word pictures a warship's single devastating volley, every gun at the same moment. A polemic is a sustained campaign rather than one salvo: it keeps prosecuting its case against a position, page after page. A broadside empties its guns; a polemic keeps arguing.

    screed: A screed is a long, heated attack that has stopped policing itself — grievances pile up faster than reasons, and discipline goes. A polemic runs just as hot but keeps its case in order: however aggressive, it is still building an argument toward a verdict. Calling a piece a screed denies it that control.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    endorsement: public backing of a position rather than an assault on it

    peace offering: a gesture meant to end a fight, not win it

    Where it sits · how heated the attack becomes

    complaint → polemic → diatribe

    complaint vs. polemic: A complaint is milder than a polemic — one grievance, aired and done, with no campaign behind it.

    diatribe vs. polemic: A diatribe goes further than a polemic: the argument dissolves into pure fury, while a polemic, however aggressive, is still building a case.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    formal / critical

    Polemic labels a piece by its posture: sustained attack on a position. A rebuttal answers an argument actually made; a complaint airs one grievance; a diatribe has lost its temper entirely. Calling a work a polemic concedes its force while warning it will not weigh both sides.

    In 1721 a London printer sold a forty-page pamphlet with a simple target: coffee. Its anonymous author did not merely grumble. Page by page, he built his case — coffee ruined sleep, emptied purses, bred idle talk — each chapter ending with a demand that the city's coffee-houses be shut. Historians now read the pamphlet as a classic polemic: aggressive from its first line, aimed at one position, and argued all the way through. What keeps it out of the crank file is its order. However hot the tone runs, the author numbers his charges and answers his critics by name. The coffee-houses, for the record, survived him comfortably.
