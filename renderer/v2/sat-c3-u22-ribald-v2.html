Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Ribald means coarsely, crudely humorous.

See the word in context

The retirement roast turned ribald by the second toast, and the honoree's grandchildren were steered early to the dessert table.

The committee translating the Roman comedies kept deadlocking: rendered politely, the ribald puns lost their whole point; rendered honestly, the edition would need a plain brown wrapper and a locked cabinet.

Ribald is coarseness in service of comedy — jokes, songs, and stories that work blue. The humor is what separates it from vulgar or obscene, which need no punch line; if nobody is supposed to laugh, it is not ribald.

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    bawdy: Bawdy means cheerfully indecent in the tavern-song tradition — good-natured, winking, meant for a room that wants to laugh. Ribald covers the same blue territory but runs coarser and more daring: a bawdy verse teases, while ribald humor barrels past the line grinning. Both live or die by the joke.

    earthy: Earthy means frank about bodies and appetites in a natural, unembarrassed way — a farmer's bluntness rather than a dirty joke. Ribald turns that frankness into comedy and pushes it toward the indecent on purpose. Earthy talk need not be funny or crude at all; ribald must be both.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    prim: carefully proper and easily scandalized

    genteel: polished, polite, and a little starchy

    Where it sits · how far past decency it goes

    naughty → ribald → obscene

    naughty vs. ribald: Naughty is milder than ribald — a wink of mischief that stays fit for the greeting-card aisle.

    obscene vs. ribald: Obscene is harsher than ribald: ribald humor works blue for laughs, while the obscene has crossed the line whether anyone laughs or not.

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      informal / literary

      Ribald praises as much as it blames — bawdy tavern humor with craft in it. Use it when coarseness is the joke; when the coarseness stops being funny you want vulgar or obscene, and when it tramples the sacred you have arrived at this unit's profane.

      The folklorist spent two summers recording harvest songs in the river valleys, and one winter discovering that no press would print a third of them. The trouble was not the melodies. Whole verses were ribald — field hands trading crude jokes about bodies and wedding nights, each stanza rowdier than the last and every chorus built to make the wagon crews roar. Her university editor proposed asterisks. The singers she had recorded proposed, cheerfully, that the editor spend a week baling hay. In the end she published two volumes: one polite, one plainly labeled. The labeled volume, she noted in the preface, outsold the polite one four to one — laughter, coarse or not, being the better salesman.
