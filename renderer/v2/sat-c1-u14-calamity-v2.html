calamity

/kuh-LAM-ih-tee/

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Calamity means an event that causes great and often sudden damage or distress.

See the word in context

The wildfire that swept through the foothill town of Pine Ridge was a calamity no one had prepared for, reducing three hundred homes to ash in a single afternoon.

When the fishing fleet was caught in a sudden ice storm off the northern coast, the calamity cost the village nearly half its boats and left dozens of families without their livelihood.

The storm strikes suddenly and causes enormous damage and distress: boats destroyed and livelihoods lost. That combination of sudden, serious, widespread harm is exactly what makes an event a calamity rather than a minor setback.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    disaster: A disaster is any event that causes great harm or loss, from a failed harvest to a flood, and it is the broadest of these words. A calamity is a disaster with the dial turned up: the word stresses how sudden the blow is and how much distress it spreads.

    catastrophe: A catastrophe is a disaster of terrible, sweeping scale, and it can sound even more final and total than a calamity: after a catastrophe, little is left to save. A calamity leans on the suddenness of the blow and the human distress left behind.

    tragedy: A tragedy is an event defined by deep sadness and suffering, often involving death; the word points at grief. A calamity points at damage and disruption: homes lost, livelihoods wrecked. A calamity can also be a tragedy, but its focus is destruction, not sorrow.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    blessing: something that brings good fortune

    windfall: an unexpected piece of good luck

    Where it sits · the scale of the damage done

    mishap → calamity → cataclysm

    mishap vs. calamity: A mishap is a small accident, like spilling paint on the floor, while a calamity causes serious, widespread damage and distress.

    cataclysm vs. calamity: A calamity devastates a town or a community, but a cataclysm is even bigger: a violent upheaval that can reshape an entire region or society.

      Section 3

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      Neutral / slightly formal, common in news and history writing

      Calamity often appears in phrases like 'a national calamity' and 'the calamity that followed.' It is far stronger than 'problem' or 'mishap' and is reserved for events with serious, widespread harm. Its near twin 'catastrophe' is largely interchangeable, though 'catastrophe' can sound even more final and total.

Dairy farmers poured milk into ditches because no truck could reach them; the county's schools, shops, and clinics went dark for eleven days. Nobody was killed, yet the newspapers did not reach for a mild word: they called the storm a calamity, and the name stuck. What earned the word was the speed of the blow and the breadth of the misery -- one night of ice, and a month of hardship for forty thousand people. By March the poles were reset and the lights were back, and Harlan County set about the ordinary work of a hard spring.
