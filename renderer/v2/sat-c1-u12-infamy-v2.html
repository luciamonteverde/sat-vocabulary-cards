infamy

/IN-fuh-mee/

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Infamy means the state of being widely known for something bad — a lasting reputation earned by a disgraceful act.

See the word in context

The con artist's infamy followed her across three states, where victims still recognized her face from the evening news.

The captain's decision to abandon his sinking ship ahead of the passengers earned him lasting infamy in maritime history.

He is remembered widely and permanently for a shameful act — famous for exactly the wrong reason — which is what infamy means.

    Accuracy

    “Does this word fit the meaning exactly, or does a neighbour fit better?”

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    disgrace: Disgrace is the everyday loss of respect that follows a shameful act — a student caught cheating is in disgrace for a while, and then life moves on. Infamy is disgrace scaled up and made permanent: the whole world knows the deed, and the bad name outlives the news cycle.

    dishonor: Dishonor is the formal loss of honor — the register of soldiers, oaths, and family names, where a code has been betrayed. Infamy needs no code, only an audience: it is the state of being widely and lastingly famous for a disgraceful deed.

    shame: Shame is what the wrongdoer feels — the hot, private sense of lost standing. Infamy lives entirely in other people's knowledge: a person can feel no shame at all and still live in infamy, remembered everywhere for the disgraceful thing they did.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    honor: high public respect

    renown: widespread fame for good reasons

    Where it sits · how crushing the bad name becomes

    disrepute → infamy → ignominy

    disrepute vs. infamy: A café with sloppy service may slide into disrepute, but infamy means being widely and lastingly known for something truly bad.

    ignominy vs. infamy: Infamy is lasting fame for a bad deed; ignominy adds a crushing sense of public humiliation and total loss of honor.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Formal / historical & journalistic

    Infamy names the state of being famous for something bad, and it tends to be lasting — 'a date which will live in infamy.' Its related adjective form, notorious (taught in another lesson of this unit), describes the person or place itself: a notorious outlaw lives in infamy. Don't use infamy for mere unpopularity; it implies a genuinely disgraceful deed the world remembers.

    The forger Han van Meegeren wanted fame, and in the end he got the other thing. For years his fake Vermeers hung in national museums while critics praised brushwork the old master never touched. When the fraud unraveled in 1945, his name traveled farther in six months than his paintings had in a lifetime. Newspapers on four continents printed his face, and schoolbooks still use his story as the cautionary example. That is the peculiar shape of infamy: the whole world learns your name, and keeps it, for exactly the reason you wished it would not.
