dirge

/DURJ/ - rhymes with urge

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Dirge means a mournful song, especially for a funeral.

See the word in context

The organist ended the memorial with a dirge, each phrase slower than the last, until the final chord faded like a breath.

At a New Orleans jazz funeral the band plays a dirge on the way to the cemetery — horns low, drums muffled, feet at a walk — and then breaks into joyful, strutting music for the march back to town.

Dirge is more exact than 'sad song': it names music composed for mourning the dead, or anything that moves at that grieving pace. Call a pop ballad a dirge and you are saying it drags like a funeral.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    threnody: A threnody is a poem or song of mourning — the literary cousin, as much at home on the page as in the air. A dirge is music first: the slow, grieving piece played for the dead. Poets publish threnodies; bands and organists play dirges.

    eulogy: A eulogy is the speech given at a funeral, praising the person who died. A dirge mourns in music instead of words: the slow, grieving melody composed for the dead. Both belong to the funeral, but the eulogy is delivered from the lectern and the dirge is played.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    anthem: a rousing song of pride or celebration

    jingle: a light, catchy tune selling something cheerful

    Where it sits · how formally the music mourns death

    lament → dirge → requiem

    lament vs. dirge: A lament is milder than a dirge — any cry or song of sorrow counts, while a dirge is music made for death itself.

    requiem vs. dirge: A requiem goes further than a dirge — not one mournful song but an entire mass for the dead, an hour or more of formal mourning.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    neutral / literary

    Dirge is the precise word for mourning music — at a funeral, or for anything that moves at a funeral's pace ('the anthem dragged like a dirge'). Its unit-mate knell is the bell that announces the death, not the song that follows; and elegiac describes a mourning tone in any art, while a dirge is a piece of music itself.

    In New Orleans, a brass-band funeral moves in two acts, and the first act begins the moment the procession leaves the church. On the slow walk toward the cemetery, the band plays a dirge — a grieving hymn taken at half tempo, trombones dragging every phrase toward the ground. Nobody dances yet. The music is for the dead, and the marchers keep their steps to its heavy beat. Only after the burial does the snare drum crack, the tempo double, and the second act begin: parasols up, handkerchiefs spinning, the street given back to the living. Musicians in the city say you can hear the whole shape of grief in one afternoon — first the music made for death, then the music made for surviving it.
