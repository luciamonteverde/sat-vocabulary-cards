fetter

/FET-er/ - rhymes with better

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Fetter means to chain or restrain.

See the word in context

Superstition fettered the whaling crew as surely as any gale: no sailing on Fridays, no whistling on deck, no renaming a boat.

One early collapse fettered the engineer for a decade: she proposed only short, cautious bridges and twice declined the river commission, until a mentor finally pried the old failure's grip off her drafting table.

Fetter keeps its picture of leg irons: whatever fetters you — fear, tradition, an actual chain — hangs on you and drags at every step. It is heavier and more vivid than limit, and it blames the restraint, not the person wearing it.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    shackle: A shackle is the literal iron cuff, and to shackle is to lock it on — the word stays close to hardware even in figurative use. Fetter shares the picture of leg irons but ranges more freely: fear, tradition, and habit can all fetter a person, hanging on and dragging at every step.

    encumber: To encumber is to weigh someone down with baggage or burdens, making every step slower and more awkward. Fetter is harsher and blames the restraint itself: what fetters you chains you like leg irons, and the word carries a blame the neutral load of encumbrance does not.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    free: to take the restraints off entirely

    release: to let go of what was held

    Where it sits · how badly progress is slowed or stopped

    hobble → fetter → paralyze

    hobble vs. fetter: Hobble is gentler than fetter — a hobbled walker still gets there, late and limping; a fettered one drags chains at every step.

    paralyze vs. fetter: Paralyze goes beyond fetter: fettered plans still creep forward, link by link, while paralyzed ones cannot move at all.

    Word Choice

    literary

    Fetter is the vivid, literary choice: it keeps its picture of leg irons, so use it when restraints hang on someone and drag — fear, tradition, or actual chains. For neutral prose about rules and budgets, constrain (this unit) serves; circumscribe (this unit) draws the formal boundary.
