buttress

/BUH-tris/ - the stress falls on BUH

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Buttress means to support or strengthen something.

See the word in context

Volunteers buttressed the bookmobile's overloaded shelves with steel brackets before the winter routes began.

Shaky at the podium, the freshman senator was buttressed by her staff's binder: every question from the panel met a tabbed page, and by the second hour her voice had stopped wavering.

Buttress is the exact word because it means to support or strengthen something so it can hold up under pressure — the binder does not replace her answers or give them for her; it stands behind her and takes part of the strain, which is precisely the job the word names.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    reinforce: Reinforce is the general engineering word for adding strength anywhere — more steel in the concrete, more staff on a shift, more evidence behind a claim. Buttress is more pointed: it braces one particular thing that faces real pressure, standing behind it and shouldering part of the strain.

    shore up: To shore up is emergency propping for something already failing — a levee about to breach, a firm about to fold. Buttress does not wait for failure: you buttress something while it is still sound so that it can bear more when the pressure comes.

    bolster: Bolster boosts confidence, morale, or numbers as readily as structures — a good scrimmage bolsters a team's nerve. Buttress is the firmer, more structural word: it implies bracing a thing against real pressure, whether that thing is a wall, a case, or a nervous witness.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    weaken: to drain strength out instead of adding it

    topple: to bring the whole structure down

    Where it sits · how lasting and how complete the reinforcement

    prop up → buttress → fortify

    prop up vs. buttress: Propping up is the quick, temporary version — a broom handle under a sagging shelf — and it stops short of buttressing, which builds lasting support into the thing.

    fortify vs. buttress: Fortify goes further than buttress — buttressing strengthens a weak point so it holds, while fortifying hardens the whole thing against outright attack.

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      formal

      Buttress does double duty: literal masonry and figurative support for claims, cases, and confidence, and it always suggests bracing something that faces real pressure. Its unit-mate stabilize stops a thing from getting worse; buttress adds strength so it can bear more. In casual speech, back up or support is plainer.
