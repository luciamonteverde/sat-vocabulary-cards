What It Means

Surmise means to infer something from little evidence.

See the word in context

When the elevator kept stopping at three, Rosa surmised that the new tenant was moving in load by load.

No log survived the wreck, but the salvage divers surmised that the ship had been outbound: the boots in the hold were unworn, the medicine chests still sealed, the winter coats untouched in June.

Surmise is the honest verb for this conclusion — drawn from scraps, stated with care, and one storm-soaked ledger away from being overturned. Deduce would claim a firmer chain of reasoning than three clues can carry; guess would ignore the clues entirely. A surmise stands exactly in between.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    infer: To infer is to draw a conclusion from evidence — any evidence, thin or overwhelming; the word itself says nothing about how much. Surmise is the special case at the thin end: a surmise is an inference built on scraps and held loosely, because so little is holding it up.

    gather: Gather is the offhand, informal word for reading hints — 'I gather you're leaving' — a mild conclusion picked up in passing, nothing checked and nothing chained together. A surmise takes the hints more seriously: it weighs each scrap and offers, carefully, the conclusion they point to.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    know: to hold a fact with certainty

    establish: to settle a question with proof

    Where it sits · how firmly the clues chain together

    guess → surmise → deduce

    guess vs. surmise: A pure guess is weaker than a surmise: a guess may rest on nothing at all, while a surmise at least grows from a few real clues.

    deduce vs. surmise: Deduce goes further than surmise: a deduction locks each clue into a firm chain of reasoning, while a surmise leaps carefully from scraps.

    Word Choice

    literary / formal

    Surmise is the word for a conclusion balanced on scraps — a guess with just enough evidence to stand. Deduce claims a firm chain of reasoning; posit, from this same unit, puts a claim forward deliberately rather than arriving at one hesitantly.

    The shoebox from Aunt Ruth's attic held no diary, only fragments: three ticket stubs, a pressed flower, and one postcard signed with the letter M. From these scraps, Nora could only surmise the outline of a story. The stubs were all for the same island steamer, in three different springs; the flower was a variety that grows on the island the steamer serves. So she offered her conclusion carefully, holding it loosely: Ruth had crossed each year to meet someone, and M had mattered. Proof was another matter. But when Nora found the island's name inked faintly on the box lid, the thin little theory grew a spine.
