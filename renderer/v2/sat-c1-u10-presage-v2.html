What It Means

Presage means an omen or warning sign that something — often something bad — is coming.

See the word in context

The analyst read the sudden spike in unusual trading as a presage of the market turmoil that followed weeks later.

To the cardiologist, the patient's recurring dizziness was a presage of the far more serious heart trouble that showed up on the scans a month later.

The dizziness arrived first and warned of the larger illness to come, making it a presage — an early sign pointing toward a future, unwelcome event.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    omen: An omen is a sign read as foretelling good or evil to come, with a superstitious flavor — comets, black cats, spilled salt. A presage is a warning sign that needs no magic: an analyst can read a trading spike as a presage of turmoil. When fate and folklore hover over the sign, omen is the word.

    harbinger: A harbinger is a forerunner that arrives and announces what follows — the first robin as a harbinger of spring. It is a grand, literary word, and the harbinger is itself the leading edge of what is coming. A presage is a sign to be read and interpreted, pointing at a coming event rather than ushering it in.

    red flag: A red flag is the everyday, conversational term for a warning sign — you spot red flags in a contract or a first conversation. A presage does the same pointing-ahead work in elevated written prose: news analysis, history, literature. Same job, different register.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    aftermath: what follows an event, rather than what warns of it

    hindsight: understanding an event only after it has happened

    Where it sits · how clearly the sign points ahead

    hint → presage → portent

    hint vs. presage: A hint merely suggests that something might happen; a presage is a clearer warning sign pointing toward a coming event.

    portent vs. presage: Portent is stronger and more dramatic than presage — it signals something momentous, even earth-shaking, on the horizon.

    Word Choice

    Formal / literary

    Presage usually appears in written, elevated prose — news analysis, history, literature. In everyday speech, people say 'warning sign' or 'red flag' instead. As a noun it is almost always 'a presage of' something; the same word can also be a verb ('dark clouds presaged the storm'), but here you are learning the noun.

    In the summer of 1930, a modest bank in Nashville quietly closed its doors, and most newspapers gave the failure a single paragraph. Economic historians now treat that closure as a presage of the collapse that followed, a warning visible to anyone who understood how tightly the country's small banks were bound to one another. The signs required interpretation, not prophecy. Depositors elsewhere began withdrawing savings within weeks, and each withdrawal weakened the next town's bank. By the time the crisis had a name, the men who had read the first closure correctly had long since moved their money.
