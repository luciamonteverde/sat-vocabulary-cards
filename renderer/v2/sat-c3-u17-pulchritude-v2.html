Meaning

What It Means

Pulchritude means physical beauty.

See the word in context

Renaissance sonnets catalog pulchritude feature by feature — brow, lip, and curl — as if beauty were an inventory.

The art history lecturer put two portraits side by side and asked which sitter possessed more pulchritude; the class laughed at the word first, then argued for an hour about symmetry, lighting, and noses.

Pulchritude works here because the question is strictly about physical beauty — the faces themselves, not charm, wit, or style. It is the formal, faintly comic word for good looks and nothing else, which is exactly what the lecturer was isolating.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    beauty: Beauty is the everyday, all-purpose word, and it ranges wide — a face, a proof, a sunset, or a kindness can all have it. Pulchritude means physical beauty only, and wears a powdered wig saying so: formal, faintly comic, reached for when a writer wants grandness or an arched eyebrow.

    comeliness: Comeliness is old-fashioned, quiet, pleasing attractiveness — agreeable looks that draw no crowd. Pulchritude is grander on both counts: the looks themselves are striking, and the word is showier, used half-seriously for effect. A kind neighbor's face has comeliness; a matinee idol's profile earns the word pulchritude.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    homeliness: plainness of face

    ugliness: the blunt opposite

    Where it sits · how striking the good looks are

    cuteness → prettiness → pulchritude

    cuteness vs. pulchritude: Cuteness charms in a small, dimpled way — it sits far below pulchritude.

    prettiness vs. pulchritude: Prettiness goes further than cuteness but stops short of pulchritude, the head-turning beauty sculptors put in marble.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    very formal, often tongue-in-cheek

    Pulchritude is beauty in a powdered wig: writers reach for it half-seriously, to be grand or gently ironic about good looks. It names physical beauty only — never inner beauty, elegance of design, or charm. In plain prose, beauty does the work; pulchritude is for when the sentence should raise an eyebrow.

    Studio publicists of the 1930s never said an actor was merely good-looking. Press releases reached instead for pulchritude, a marble word rolled out to make a profile sound like a national monument. The term promised one thing only — the face and figure — and said nothing about talent, wit, or kindness. Reviewers of the era used it with a raised eyebrow, half admiring the star and half teasing the studio for its trumpets. Even today, a critic who calls someone's pulchritude 'undeniable' is usually smiling while typing.
