intransigent

/in-TRAN-suh-juhnt/ - the stress falls on TRAN

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Intransigent means refusing to compromise or change a position.

See the word in context

Both other landlords trimmed their asking prices; the third stayed intransigent, and his corner lot sat empty for years.

Four editors in a row suggested cutting the nine-hundred-page manuscript by a third. The novelist proved intransigent, and the book finally shipped with every last semicolon intact.

Intransigent is stronger than stubborn: the stubborn dig in against persuasion, while the intransigent reject the very idea of negotiating. In- ('not') + transigere ('to drive a deal through') — someone who will not transact.

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    unyielding: Unyielding means refusing to give ground under pressure -- the position simply does not move, however hard it is pushed. It can describe a wall or a will as easily as a person. Intransigent is narrower and always about negotiation: not just holding ground, but rejecting the very process of compromise.

    uncompromising: Uncompromising means holding out for all of it, every time -- and it is often praise: an uncompromising editor upholds a standard whole. Intransigent never compliments; it is the negotiator's accusation, aimed at the party that refuses to deal at all.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    yielding: giving way when pressed

    persuadable: open to a good argument

    Where it sits · how closed to persuasion it is

    firm → stubborn → intransigent

    firm vs. intransigent: Firm holds a position but can still hear reasons — it stops well short of intransigent.

    stubborn vs. intransigent: Stubborn digs in past most persuasion, yet stops short of intransigent, which refuses the very idea of compromise.

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    formal

    Intransigent is the negotiator's word — for the party that rejects compromise itself, not just your latest offer. Stubborn is character; intransigent is posture at the table, and it shades critical: calling someone intransigent all but accuses them of bad faith.
