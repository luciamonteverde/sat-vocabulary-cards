licentious

/lahy-SEN-shuhs/ - the stress falls on SEN

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Licentious means ignoring accepted rules of moral or lawful conduct.

See the word in context

Critics condemned the novel's licentious hero, who treats promises, bans, and borders alike as suggestions.

The academy's third headmaster inherited a licentious student body: seniors ran a betting ring from the bell tower, the curfew was a rumor, and the honor code had gone unsigned for years. His first assembly ended when the fire alarm was pulled—cheerfully—by the student council president.

Licentious is graver than 'wild' or 'unruly': it describes people who ignore accepted moral and legal limits as a way of life, not children who merely misbehave on a given afternoon.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    lawless: Lawless means ignoring the law specifically — a lawless town has no working sheriff. Licentious casts a wider net: the licentious ignore moral limits as well as legal ones, treating promises, decency, and statutes alike as optional. Lawlessness breaks the codes on the books; licentiousness breaks the unwritten ones too.

    dissolute: Dissolute describes a life dissolved into self-indulgence — drink, gambling, idle pleasure — a private slide into excess. Licentious is the wider public charge: not simply chasing your own pleasures, but treating the moral and legal limits that bind everyone as optional.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    upright: holding firmly to honest, moral conduct

    disciplined: keeping oneself within the rules by habit

    Where it sits · how gravely the rules are defied

    mischievous → unruly → licentious

    mischievous vs. licentious: Mischievous stops far short of licentious — mischief bends small rules for fun and means little harm.

    unruly vs. licentious: Unruly stops short of licentious — the unruly resist control, while the licentious ignore moral and lawful limits altogether.

      Section 3

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      formal / disapproving

      Licentious is a formal word of moral condemnation — historians and critics use it for courts, ports, and characters that treat law and morality as optional. It is not the word for ordinary naughtiness, and note the trap in its own family: licensed means officially permitted; licentious means behaving as if no permission were ever needed.

      The pamphlet that scandalized Lisbon in 1783 devoted forty pages to the Marquis of Veiga's set. Its catalogue was methodical. Debts of honor left unpaid to tradesmen who could not refuse them. Duels fought over insults invented for the occasion. A wedding vow, the writer noted acidly, 'observed chiefly in its breach,' and a magistrate's summons used to light a cigar. The author needed a word equal to the whole list, and chose licentious: not merely wild, he wrote, but a set of men for whom no rule — of law, of honor, of common decency — carried any force at all. Veiga's circle reportedly found the pamphlet hilarious. The tradesmen who had gone unpaid did not.
