appeasement

/uh-PEEZ-ment/

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Appeasement means the act of pacifying or satisfying someone by giving in to their demands, especially at the cost of one's own principles.

See the word in context

At Munich in 1938, Britain's policy of appeasement handed territory to an aggressor in exchange for a promise of peace that lasted less than a year.

The manager's appeasement of his rudest client — waiving fees and bumping her to the front of every queue — only taught her that complaining loudly always worked.

The sentence shows appeasement's core bargain: the manager buys temporary calm by surrendering fair rules, sacrificing principle to satisfy someone's demands.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    placation: Placation is the soothing of someone's anger in the moment — a free dessert, a quick apology. Appeasement goes further: it buys calm by surrendering one's own principles to the angry party's demands. You can placate without giving up anything that matters; appeasement always pays with principle.

    conciliation: Conciliation is a mutual effort to rebuild goodwill — both parties soften, both move toward the middle. Appeasement is one-sided: a single party gives and gives, hoping the demands will simply stop. Conciliation repairs a relationship between equals; appeasement feeds an appetite.

    concession: A concession is a single thing yielded in a negotiation, and trading concessions is how fair bargains get made. Appeasement is the whole strategy of yielding again and again while asking nothing back. One concession can be wise; a policy of appeasement only teaches the other side to demand more.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    defiance: Defiance openly refuses the demands that appeasement would satisfy.

    resistance: Resistance pushes back against pressure instead of buying peace by giving in.

    Where it sits · how completely one side gives in

    compromise → appeasement → capitulation

    compromise vs. appeasement: A compromise is a neutral trade where both sides give something up, while appeasement is lopsided — one side gives, hoping the other will simply stop demanding.

    capitulation vs. appeasement: Capitulation is a complete surrender on every point, going beyond appeasement, which still imagines it is purchasing peace rather than admitting defeat.

      Section 3

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      formal / historical-political

      Appeasement is almost always disapproving: it implies buying calm by sacrificing principle, and in historical writing it points specifically at the failed 1930s policy toward Nazi Germany. Don't use it for an ordinary fair compromise — a compromise trades; appeasement surrenders.
