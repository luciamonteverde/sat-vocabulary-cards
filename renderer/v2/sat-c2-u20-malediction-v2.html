Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Malediction means a spoken curse that wishes harm on its target.

See the word in context

The soccer manager's touchline malediction — may every referee's phone die in a strange city — made even the fourth official laugh.

When the council voted to pave over the spring, ninety-year-old Sefa stood at the back of the hall and delivered her malediction in the old language: may the new fountain rust, may its water taste of coins, and may every councilman's garden crave rain. The clerk did not enter it in the minutes, but nobody who heard it forgot a word.

Malediction is the exact word for Sefa's speech, because a malediction is a spoken curse — male-, badly, plus dict-, said: ill fortune wished out loud onto a target. An expletive is only steam escaping; an insult wounds the present moment; a malediction aims at the future, and means it.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    curse: A curse is the everyday word for ill fortune wished on someone — you can mutter one in a parking lot. A malediction is the same wish delivered with formality and style, the word of ballads and old courtrooms. Every malediction is a curse; only the ceremonious ones earn the longer name.

    hex: A hex is a curse worked as a spell — it wants ritual, a charm, candles, and the words are optional. A malediction is entirely spoken: harm wished out loud onto its target, no ritual required. Objects and ceremony make a hex; pronouncement alone makes a malediction.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    blessing: good called down instead of harm

    well-wish: harm's opposite, spoken aloud

    Where it sits · how lasting the intended harm is

    insult → malediction → damnation

    insult vs. malediction: An insult is milder than a malediction — it wounds the present moment and is done; a malediction wishes lasting harm onto its target's future.

    damnation vs. malediction: Damnation goes further than a malediction — a malediction wishes ruin on someone; damnation is that ruin pronounced final, with no appeal left.

      The folklorist's tape recorder nearly missed it. At the end of a long interview about boat-building, the oldest netmaker on the island straightened, set down her awl, and performed the malediction her grandmother had taught her against gear thieves. It ran four full sentences, wishing torn sails, empty holds, and forgotten graves on any hand that lifted another family's nets, and it had a meter you could tap your foot to. She was not angry at anyone that afternoon, she explained; the words were an heirloom, kept polished in case they were ever needed. You could grumble at a thief any day, she said, but this was said in full, standing up, the way her grandmother had insisted it be said.
