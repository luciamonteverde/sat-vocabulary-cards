vilify

/VIL-uh-fye/ - the stress falls on VIL

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Vilify means to attack someone's reputation with cruel or unjust words.

See the word in context

When the factory closed, its former owner used every interview to vilify the union, blaming the very workers who had taken pay cuts to keep the doors open.

After the heiress left her fortune to a stranger, the disappointed cousins set out to vilify him — hinting at forgery, inventing a criminal past — until a court examined every charge and found nothing.

Vilify is stronger than criticize and dirtier than mock: it means dragging a name through the mud, often with unjust or invented charges. The Latin root vilis, 'worthless,' is the point — to vilify someone is to work at making them look worthless.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    malign: Malign means to speak harmful, usually unfair words about someone, and it stays small and scattered — a resentful aside here, a whisper passed along there. Vilify is bigger and louder: a sustained public campaign to make its target look worthless. Being maligned stings; being vilified can ruin a name.

    smear: Smear means to spread a damaging accusation — true or invented — and ride it; it is journalism's word for a campaign built on charges. Vilify aims wider than any one accusation: it attacks the whole character. A smear pins a charge on you; to vilify is to blacken the man himself.

    defame: Defame is the legal term for injuring a reputation with false statements — the word of courtrooms, lawsuits, and damages. Vilify describes the cruelty of the attack, not its legal standing: you can vilify someone viciously without ever being sued. Defamation is an offense; vilifying is an act.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    praise: to speak well of someone openly

    commend: to praise someone formally

    Where it sits · how monstrous the target is painted

    criticize → vilify → demonize

    criticize vs. vilify: Criticism can be fair and even friendly; it stops far short of vilifying, which sets out to blacken the person rather than weigh the work.

    demonize vs. vilify: Demonize goes further than vilify: the vilified man is made to look worthless, while the demonized one is made to look like evil itself.

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    formal / journalistic

    Vilify is the act; this unit's calumny is often the ammunition — the invented story used to do it. Choose vilify over 'badmouth' when the attack is public, sustained, and aims at the person's character; choose 'defame' when you mean the legal offense.

    After the cup final, blame settled on one person: the referee who had waved off the late goal. Within a day, callers and columnists set out to vilify him -- not to dispute the call, but to take apart the man: his competence, his honesty, his right to ever work again. The league's replay review, published that Friday, found the call correct. It changed little. The campaign had never really been about the goal; a correct decision was beside the point once the target was his character. He officiated again in spring, to thinner boos, and told one interviewer the strangest part: 'Nobody ever argued the rule with me. They argued that I was worthless.'
