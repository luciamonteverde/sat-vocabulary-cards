extol

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Extol means to praise enthusiastically — to lift something up in words, warmly and at full voice.

See the word in context

Coach Imani spends the first practice extolling the humble hill sprint, promising her freshmen it will repay every groan they give it.

At the co-op's annual meeting, the produce manager stood to extol the ugly carrots — knobbly, cheap, and twice as sweet — until the board voted to double the order.

Extolling is praise with a pulpit and a purpose: the manager doesn't just like the carrots, he lifts them up before an audience, virtue by virtue, to move that audience to act. That upward, out-loud push is the word's whole gesture — you extol in words, and always toward other ears.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    laud: To laud is to praise formally, often on the record — a citation lauds. Extol shares the formality but adds fervor and push: extolling praises at length, virtue by virtue, to win the listener over.

    tout: To tout is to promote something loudly, often with an eye on the sale — the pitch matters more than the truth. Extol praises just as loudly but in earnest: an extoller believes every word.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    disparage: to talk something down as minor or worthless

    belittle: to make something sound smaller and less impressive than it is

    Where it sits · how hard the praise pushes upward

    commend → extol → glorify

    commend vs. extol: Commend is milder than extol: a commendation notes merit in a sentence and stops, while extolling settles in — praise built point by point, at full voice.

    glorify vs. extol: Glorify is stronger than extol: extolling praises a real thing for real virtues, while glorifying inflates past the evidence, polishing its subject toward the mythic.

      Section 3

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      formal / persuasive

      Extol is praise on its feet, making a case: you extol to move an audience — a committee, a crowd, a readership. Its unit-mates divide the territory: exalt raises its subject's rank, acclaim is the applause coming back, and rhapsodize gushes for the pleasure of gushing. Extol works when the praise has a purpose; nobody extols quietly, and nobody extols by accident.

      The first forty pages of 'In Defense of Cabbage' contain no recipes at all. Instead the chef spends them extolling her favorite vegetable, point by point: its price, its patience in a cold cellar, the seven distinct textures she insists it contains, its centuries of quiet service in a dozen cuisines. The tone is not salesmanship — she has nothing to gain; cabbage is not sponsoring anyone — but conviction at full volume. Reviewers admit the praise is contagious. 'By page thirty,' one wrote, 'I had put down the book, gone out, and bought three heads of it.' The recipes, when they finally arrive, feel almost like an afterthought.
