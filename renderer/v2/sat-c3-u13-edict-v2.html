edict

/EE-dikt/ - the stress falls on EE

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Edict means an order or decree issued by an authority.

See the word in context

By royal edict, no lantern could burn in the harbor town past the curfew bell.

The new director's first edict reached every branch library by Monday morning: no fines, effective immediately. Clerks unplugged the cash drawers, taped the notice to the desks, and grumbled that nobody had asked them.

An edict comes from above, whole and finished — no debate, no vote, no comment period. That top-down finality is why the word often carries a faint eye-roll: calling the memo an edict hints that the boss has been playing emperor.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    decree: A decree is the edict's near-twin, an order issued whole from a throne or a bench — the word stays in royal and judicial robes. Edict travels further down: any authority's take-it-or-else order, and in offices it adds an eye-roll at a boss playing emperor.

    proclamation: A proclamation is a public announcement, made to be heard widely, and it need not bind anyone — holidays and honors get proclaimed. An edict commands: it arrives from above, finished, and must be obeyed. A proclamation informs the crowd; an edict tells the crowd what it will do.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    plea: an appeal sent up from below, carrying no power

    petition: a formal request that authority may refuse

    Where it sits · how much authority backs the command

    suggestion → directive → edict

    suggestion vs. edict: A suggestion is the mildest word here — it can be declined with a smile, while an edict cannot be declined at all.

    directive vs. edict: A directive stops short of an edict: it instructs and expects compliance, but an edict carries the full take-it-or-else weight of the authority who issued it.

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      formal, often faintly critical

      Edict fits orders that arrive from above, whole and unappealable — emperors, courts, and, with an eye-roll, bosses. A directive instructs; a decree is its near-twin in royal robes. Call a workplace rule an edict and you are quietly noting who has been playing emperor.
