congenital

/kun-JEN-ih-tul/

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Congenital means present from birth, usually describing a condition or trait.

See the word in context

The foal's congenital heart defect was audible to the vet within an hour of its birth.

A calf born on the county farm cannot straighten its left foreleg. The vet's notes record that the joint was already fused at delivery, that the mother's pregnancy included a bout of fever in its fourth month, and that no animal in the calf's line has ever shown the trait. The condition is therefore congenital but not inherited: it was there at birth, caused by an illness in the womb rather than by a gene.

The defect already existed at the moment of birth, which makes it congenital, and the note that no ancestor ever had it shows why congenital does not have to mean passed down.

    Accuracy

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    inborn: Inborn is the plain-English way of saying a trait was there from birth -- an inborn stubbornness, an inborn knack. Congenital is the medical register of the same when-claim, the word charts and textbooks use for conditions present at birth, whatever their cause.

    innate: Innate means woven into a person or animal's makeup from the start, and it usually describes capacities and temperaments -- an innate ear for music. Congenital is the clinical word, used of conditions and defects recorded at birth rather than of talents.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    acquired: developed after birth rather than present from the start

    induced: brought about later by an outside cause

    Where it sits · how precisely the cause is pinned down

    lifelong → congenital → genetic

    lifelong vs. congenital: Lifelong says only that a condition has been there as long as anyone can remember, pinning down nothing about its start, while congenital makes the precise claim that it was present on the day of birth.

    genetic vs. congenital: Genetic is a stronger and narrower claim than congenital, because it names a cause in the genes, while congenital says only when the condition appeared.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    medical and technical; neutral

    Congenital says only that something was present at birth and does not say why. Hereditary, taught in this unit, is the narrower word for traits carried down through genes, so a hereditary condition is congenital, while many congenital conditions, such as those caused by an infection or injury before birth, are not hereditary at all.

    For most of history, a child born with clouded lenses simply grew up without sharp sight, and no one knew why the world stayed blurred. Modern pediatric wards changed that. Every newborn's eyes are now checked with a simple light test before the family goes home. The test exists because congenital cataracts -- present on the day of birth rather than developing later -- can be corrected best in the first weeks of life. Timing is everything: an infant brain learns to see by seeing, so a lens cleared at six weeks lets vision develop almost normally, while the same surgery at six years comes too late. Screening programs built on this one fact have quietly saved the sight of thousands of children who would never have known what they were missing.
