What It Means

Tautology means a statement that repeats the same idea in different words.

See the word in context

The campaign slogan — 'We will win because we will not lose' — was a tautology, though the crowd cheered it like an argument.

Asked why the crops had failed, the almanac offered a tautology: 'Poor harvests occur in years when yields are low.' Farmers noticed that the sentence, however confident, told them nothing they could plant by.

A tautology repeats its own meaning in different words — saying one thing twice while appearing to say two things. It differs from a truism, which is at least a real claim, just an obvious one; a tautology cannot be false, and therefore cannot inform.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    pleonasm: Pleonasm is wordiness inside a phrase — piling on more words than the meaning needs, like 'true fact' or 'burning fire.' A tautology operates at the scale of a whole statement, one that restates itself and so cannot be false. A pleonasm wastes words; a tautology wastes the entire sentence.

    redundancy: Redundancy is the general editing fault of saying the same thing twice — repeated words, repeated meaning, anywhere in a text. A tautology is the logical version: a complete statement that is true by its own construction and therefore tells you nothing. 'Free gift' is a redundancy; 'either it will rain or it won't' is a tautology.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    contradiction: a statement that cannot be true because it defeats itself

    revelation: a statement that delivers genuinely new information

    Where it sits · how little the statement actually says

    cliche → truism → tautology

    cliche vs. tautology: A cliche is worn from overuse but can still fit the moment; it stops well short of a tautology, which adds nothing at all.

    truism vs. tautology: A truism still says something about the world, however obvious; it stops just short of a tautology, which is empty by its very wording.

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    formal / academic

    Tautology is the exact word in logic and editing for a statement that repeats itself and so cannot inform. For a phrase that is merely wordy, 'padding' will do; for an obvious but genuine claim, say 'truism.' Reserve tautology for wording that is true by construction — and therefore empty.

    The consultant's report on Brightline's mission statement ran to a single page. The statement -- 'We will succeed by achieving success' -- had hung in the lobby for six years. It is a tautology, the report said: read it forward or backward, it commits the company to nothing, because no possible future could make it false. Employees defended the line as inspiring. The consultant conceded that and pressed a different point: a claim that cannot fail cannot guide. By spring the lobby wall carried a new sentence, one specific enough to be wrong: 'Every order ships in two days.'
