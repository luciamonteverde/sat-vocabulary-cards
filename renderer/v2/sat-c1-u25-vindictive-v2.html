vindictive

/vin-DIK-tiv/

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Vindictive means having or showing a strong desire for revenge.

See the word in context

Losing the case made him vindictive: he spent the next year trying to get the opposing lawyer disbarred.

After the bakery review — two stars, 'promising but uneven' — the owner did not revise her recipes. She posted the critic's home address online, called his editor demanding he be fired, and left one-star ratings on every business his family owned. Even sympathetic regulars went quiet. A tough review deserved a tough response, they agreed, but this was something else: pure vindictive fury, aimed at punishing the man rather than fixing the bread.

The owner's campaign exists solely to punish the critic — address-posting, firing demands, retaliatory ratings — and that drive to take revenge is what vindictive means.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    vengeful: Vengeful means seeking to repay a wrong with harm, and it is the plainest name for the drive itself. Vindictive is the same drive worn as a disposition: a vindictive person keeps the score alive, punishing long after the original wrong, often coldly and within the rules.

    spiteful: Spiteful means acting from petty malice — small, mean gestures made to sting, with or without any real injury behind them. Vindictive is bigger and aimed: a vindictive campaign repays one specific wrong, and it can run patiently for years. Spite snipes; vindictiveness settles scores.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    forgiving: letting go of wrongs

    merciful: showing compassion instead of punishment

    Where it sits · how far bitterness turns into punishment

    resentful → vindictive → merciless

    resentful vs. vindictive: Resentful is weaker than vindictive: a resentful person nurses bitterness privately, while a vindictive one acts on it to punish.

    merciless vs. vindictive: Merciless is stronger than vindictive — merciless describes punishment without any restraint at all, while vindictive names the revenge-hungry motive behind it.

      Anya Petrossian's memoir devotes a chapter to Rudolf Maas, the rival she defeated for the 1978 chess title. Maas never challenged her to a rematch. Instead, she writes, he grew vindictive, and the punishment arrived slowly, through channels. He sat on invitation committees and kept her name off tournament lists. He lobbied the federation to review her eligibility, twice, on paperwork grounds. None of it was loud. Every move was procedurally correct, and every move traced back to a single evening in 1978. Petrossian marvels less at the anger than at its patience: a defeat repaid in small official acts for eleven years.
