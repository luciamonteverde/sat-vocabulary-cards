Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Reprobate means morally unprincipled; wicked.

See the word in context

Family reunions still retell Great-Uncle Amos, the reprobate of the clan, who sold his cousins shares in a gold mine that never existed and toasted their health with the proceeds.

The reprobate sea captain of the old court record makes chilling reading: he sold his own crew's rations to a passing trader mid-voyage, blamed the shortage on rats, and docked the cook's pay for the missing stores. Asked at the inquiry whether anything weighed on him, he is recorded as answering, cheerfully, 'No.'

Reprobate is the exact word for that captain, because it means morally unprincipled, wicked — and it judges the whole character, not one act. Dishonest could describe a single lie and leave the rest of the man intact; reprobate says the rot goes all the way through, worn without shame.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    villainous: Villainous is storybook wickedness, often theatrical — capes, schemes, a laugh pitched to reach the cheap seats. Reprobate is the sober, real-world verdict: settled, shameless wrongdoing worn in plain daylight, with no performance required and no audience needed.

    amoral: Amoral means without any moral sense at all — colder and more clinical than wicked, a blank where the conscience should be, with no notion of right to defy. A reprobate knows exactly what decency demands and tramples it anyway, without a flicker of shame.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    virtuous: actively good, principle by principle

    honorable: keeps a code even when the code costs

    Where it sits · how little conscience remains

    shady → corrupt → reprobate

    shady vs. reprobate: Shady is much milder than reprobate — suspicion's word, corners probably cut and questions dodged, nothing proved yet — while reprobate is a finished verdict on the whole character.

    corrupt vs. reprobate: Corrupt goes further than shady — the principles are genuinely for sale — but it stops short of reprobate, which finds no principles left to sell and no shame about the vacancy.

      Section 3

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      formal / literary, often wry

      Reprobate condemns the whole character, not one act: settled, shameless wickedness. It works as an adjective — a reprobate paymaster — and at least as often as a noun: an old reprobate. Villainous is stagier; amoral is colder. Keep the unit-mates straight by role: you reprove a person gently for an act, you repudiate a claim or a debt, you repulse an attack — but reprobate is what you call the person once the judgment on their character is final.
