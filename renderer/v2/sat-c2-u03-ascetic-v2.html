ascetic

/uh-SET-ik/ - the stress falls on SET

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Ascetic means practicing strict self-denial as a form of discipline.

See the word in context

The philosophy professor lived an ascetic life by choice: one rented room, two sets of clothes, and plain rice dinners, though her salary could buy far more.

The chess grandmaster spent the six weeks before the title match in an ascetic routine: plain meals, dawn walks, no phone, no visitors. Comfort, he told his team, dulls calculation, and he wanted his mind stripped as bare as his training table.

Ascetic is stronger than 'simple' or 'strict' because it names self-denial chosen as a discipline - giving up comfort on purpose to sharpen the mind or spirit, not merely doing without because money is short.

    Accuracy

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    abstinent: Abstinent means refraining from one particular pleasure - alcohol, sweets, screens - often for health or a vow. Ascetic sweeps far wider: an ascetic person gives up comfort across the board, as a standing discipline of self-denial, not one renounced indulgence.

    self-denying: Self-denying people give things up, often so that others can have more - the parent who skips lunch so the children eat. Ascetic self-denial points inward: comforts surrendered as a deliberate discipline to sharpen the mind or spirit, not as a sacrifice made for someone else.

    austere: Austere describes severity and plainness - an austere office, a plain gray suit, a stern manner - and it judges how something looks or feels. Ascetic names the practice behind such bareness: a person deliberately denying themselves comfort as a discipline of mind or spirit.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    indulgent: freely allowing pleasures and comforts

    hedonistic: treating pleasure as the whole point of life

    Where it sits · how deliberately comforts are given up

    sparing → spartan → ascetic

    sparing vs. ascetic: A sparing diner skips dessert now and then; ascetic describes someone who has sworn off dessert - and most other comforts - as a standing rule.

    spartan vs. ascetic: A spartan room is bare and comfortless, but the word describes the surroundings; ascetic goes further, naming a person's deliberate discipline of self-denial behind that bareness.

      Section 3

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      formal / literary

      Ascetic marks the extreme end of self-denial: comfort given up on purpose, as a discipline. Keep it apart from spartan, which describes bare conditions rather than a person's chosen practice - a cheap motel room is spartan, a hermit's chosen bare hut is ascetic. Among this unit's words, ascetic is about denying comfort, while assiduous and resolute are about applying effort.
