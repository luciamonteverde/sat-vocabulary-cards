Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Abduct means to kidnap or carry someone off by force.

See the word in context

Villagers still tell of the night raiders tried to abduct the miller's daughter, only to be driven off by every dog in the lane.

When the young violinist vanished between the concert hall and her lodgings, police first assumed she had wandered off; the torn sleeve and dropped bow by the stage door told them someone had abducted her.

Abduct is more precise than 'take' or even 'capture' because it names the seizing of a person — you abduct someone, not something — and it implies force or trickery rather than lawful custody.

    Accuracy

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    kidnap: Kidnap is the everyday word for the same crime, and it usually hints at a ransom demand to come. Abduct names the identical act — seizing and carrying off a person — but wears the formal register of the law: warrants and news columns say abducted.

    snatch: To snatch is informal and quick — a grab made in the moment, with no plan behind it. Abduct is the opposite in both register and planning: a planned seizure of a person, carried out by force or trickery and aimed at carrying the victim away. A thief snatches a purse; a person is abducted.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    release: to set a captive free

    rescue: to bring someone out of the captors' hands

    Where it sits · how completely the person's freedom is taken

    waylay → detain → abduct

    waylay vs. abduct: To waylay a traveler is only to stop him on his road — a delay, maybe a robbery; waylay stops well short of abduct, which carries the victim away entirely.

    detain vs. abduct: Guards may detain a suspect for an afternoon and let him go; detain stops short of abduct, which is seizure with no lawful authority and no promise of return.

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      formal / legal

      Abduct is the word for police reports, court filings, and news columns; kidnap is its everyday twin and usually hints at ransom. Reach for abduct when you want the formal register of the law — and remember its object is always a person, never a thing.

      Nineteenth-century port cities kept a grim open secret: sailors were routinely abducted from the waterfront to fill out short-handed crews. Boardinghouse keepers known as crimps would befriend a man fresh off a voyage, stand him a drugged drink, and deliver him unconscious to an outbound ship. The victim might wake two days from land, his mark forged on the ship's articles, legally bound for the far side of the world with no way home but the voyage itself. Newspapers protested the practice for decades, yet captains desperate for hands paid the crimps well, and harbor officials seldom asked how a crew had been gathered. Only when the law required every sailor to sign before a shipping commissioner did this trade in stolen men finally wither.
