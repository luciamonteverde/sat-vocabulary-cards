Meaning

Hear It

What It Means

Dour means stern, joyless.

See the word in context

Even at the county fair, the dour shepherd watched the prize ribbons being handed out with the same flat, unsmiling stare.

The expedition hired the dour old pilot precisely because he never joked: briefings were short, forecasts were blunt, and bad weather got the same level voice as clear skies. Nobody ever mistook his silence for doubt.

Dour describes an outward manner — stern, unsmiling, joyless — and says nothing about what is underneath; plenty of dour characters turn out generous. That is what separates it from words for inner sadness.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    grim: Grim means bleak and unsmiling, and it usually colors the outlook — grim news, grim prospects, a grim forecast. Dour attaches to the person instead: a stern, joyless manner worn on the face and in the voice. The situation is grim; the man watching it is dour.

    humorless: Humorless means unable or unwilling to find anything funny — every joke lands on stone. Dour is broader and colder: a whole manner that stays stern, unsmiling, and joyless even when no joke is anywhere in sight. A humorless clerk never laughs; a dour one chills the room.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    jolly: openly, roundly merry

    sunny: warm and bright in temperament

    Where it sits · how cold and unwelcoming the manner is

    stern → dour → forbidding

    stern vs. dour: Stern is milder than dour — a stern coach demands discipline yet may still crack a smile; dour has no smile to offer.

    forbidding vs. dour: Forbidding goes further than dour — a forbidding manner doesn't just withhold warmth, it actively warns people away.

      Apply

      Word Choice

      neutral, mildly critical

      Dour describes the surface: a stern, unsmiling, joyless manner. It passes no judgment on what's underneath — plenty of dour clerks and coaches have soft hearts. Its unit-mate morose is the inward version, genuine settled gloom. A dour lighthouse keeper may be perfectly content with his fog; a morose one is suffering in it.

      Passengers on the island ferry learn quickly not to expect small talk from Captain Ross. He is a dour man by every account: the same unsmiling nod at boarding, the same flat 'mind the step,' whether the sun is out or the swell is running high. The crossing itself can be grim in November, all gray water and warnings, but the captain is no different in July. Regulars insist he is not unkind. He remembers which child gets seasick and quietly hands the mother a seat by the window, all without a flicker of warmth crossing his face. One deckhand summed him up after twenty years: the weather changes, the man does not.
