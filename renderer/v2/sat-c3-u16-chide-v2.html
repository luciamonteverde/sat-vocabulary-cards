What It Means

Chide means to scold gently; to voice mild disapproval.

See the word in context

The yoga teacher chided us for rushing the cooldown — one raised eyebrow and a soft 'again, slowly.'

When the new hire signed his emails with just his initials, the office manager chided him in passing: 'We do full names here, friend.'

Chide is the gentlest word on the scolding shelf: mild disapproval voiced without anger, often with warmth still in it. If voices rise or the correction goes on the record, you have left chide behind for its sterner cousins.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    correct: To correct is to point out a fault neutrally, with no disapproving tone at all — just the fix, stated plainly. Chide adds mild, warm disapproval on top: the speaker really does mean the reproof, however gently it lands. A spell-checker corrects; a fond grandmother chides.

    reproach: To reproach someone is to voice mild blame with hurt feelings in it — 'you might have called.' Chide carries affection instead of injury: the chider is not wounded, just gently steering someone back to form. A friend you let down reproaches you; an aunt who catches you slouching chides you.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    encourage: urge someone on warmly

    flatter: please someone with admiring words, sincere or not

    Where it sits · how much sting the words carry

    tease → chide → scold

    tease vs. chide: Teasing is play, not correction — even gentler than chide, which does mean the disapproval.

    scold vs. chide: Scold goes further than chide: real anger in the voice, where chide keeps its warmth.

      gentle / conversational

      My beekeeping mentor, Mrs. Okafor, chided me the whole first summer — for rushing the smoker, for skipping gloves, for talking too loudly around the hives. It never once felt like being told off. She would shake her head, half-smiling, and say, 'Again, but slower this time.' The disapproval was real; she genuinely wanted the habits gone. But there was no anger in it, and no scorekeeping. When I finally cracked a frame, I braced for the storm. It never came — just the head-shake, the smile, and a quieter 'slower.' Years on, I catch myself doing the same gentle scolding with my own students, right down to the half-smile.
