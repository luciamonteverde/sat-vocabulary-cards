tenuous

/TEN-yoo-us/

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Tenuous means very weak or slight, describing a connection, argument, or claim that barely holds together.

See the word in context

The prosecutor's case rested on a tenuous link between the suspect and the scene, a single blurry photo taken from a moving bus.

In his essay, Diego argued that a medieval trade route explained a modern city's love of spicy food, but the chain of reasoning was tenuous: he offered one travel diary, a folk song, and four centuries of pure guesswork in between.

Diego's argument barely holds together, resting on a couple of thin scraps of evidence stretched across a huge gap, which is exactly what makes a claim tenuous.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    flimsy: A flimsy argument or excuse is weakly built and easily knocked down; one good question makes it collapse. Tenuous is about thinness rather than bad construction: a tenuous link barely exists at all, one thread stretched across a wide gap. Flimsy faults the building; tenuous measures how little is there.

    insubstantial: Insubstantial means lacking real substance or solid support; an insubstantial case has nothing weighty anywhere in it. Tenuous is more precise about shape: it pictures a single thin connection close to snapping, a tenuous claim or a tenuous link between two facts.

    thin: Thin, applied to evidence or a case, is the plain everyday word for not much there, as in 'the evidence is thin.' Tenuous is its formal cousin, reserved for connections and arguments so slight they barely hold together, the word a scholar or lawyer would reach for.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    solid: firmly supported and dependable

    convincing: strong enough to persuade

    Where it sits · how much supporting evidence remains

    shaky → tenuous → baseless

    shaky vs. tenuous: A shaky argument has real weaknesses but might still stand with repairs, while a tenuous one barely holds together at all.

    baseless vs. tenuous: A tenuous claim still hangs by a thin thread of evidence, but a baseless one has no support whatsoever.

    Word Choice

    formal

    Tenuous almost always describes abstract things: a tenuous link, a tenuous argument, a tenuous claim to a title. Vulnerable describes being open to harm or attack rather than describing a weak connection or argument.

    The auction catalog claimed the pocket watch had belonged to the polar explorer Elias Voss, and bidding opened at ten times the usual price. A curator named Ito asked the obvious question: what tied the watch to Voss? The answer proved tenuous — a single link, and a frail one. The seller's file held one letter, written forty years after the expedition, in which a collector recalled buying 'a watch said to be Voss's' from a man he did not name. No engraving, no photograph, no entry in the expedition's meticulous equipment logs — Ito checked all three — connected the watch to the explorer. The house withdrew the lot. As Ito put it, a chain of ownership is only as good as its links, and this chain had one, made of hearsay.
