Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Recapitulate means to summarize and restate the main points.

See the word in context

At the top of every episode the narrator recapitulates the story so far, one plot thread at a time, for listeners joining late.

At seven each morning the night nurse recapitulates her shift for the day team: which patient spiked a fever and when, which medications moved, which monitor misbehaved. A whole night on the ward fits into four spoken minutes, delivered in the same order every day, and nothing important is allowed to fall out of the telling.

Recapitulate is the exact word for that handoff, because it means to summarize and restate the main points — back over the ground, headline by headline, so the whole shape is visible at once. It is not mere repeating: a recording repeats; a recapitulation selects, orders, and restates.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    restate: Restate means to say a point again, usually in fresh words, to make it clearer or firmer — one point at a time, with no promise of order or coverage. Recapitulate is systematic where restate is piecemeal: it walks back through all the main points, in order, so the whole holds together.

    go over: Go over is the casual phrase for revisiting material — informally, in any order, at whatever length the moment allows. Recapitulate is its formal cousin, at home in lectures, briefings, and closing arguments, and it promises an ordered, point-by-point return through everything that matters.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    drag out: stretch the telling far past its main points

    pad out: swell it with filler no listener needs

    Where it sits · how thoroughly the material gets revisited

    touch on → sum up → recapitulate

    touch on vs. recapitulate: Touch on is a much slighter pass than recapitulate — a speaker names a topic in a phrase and moves along, stating nothing of substance about it.

    sum up vs. recapitulate: Sum up goes further than touch on — the gist gets stated squarely — but it stops short of recapitulate, which walks back through the points in order and restates each one.

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    formal / academic

    Recapitulate is the formal word for walking back through points already made — lectures, briefings, closing arguments. In casual speech, sum up or go over is lighter. Note the lanes among its unit-mates: refurbish restores an object and rejuvenate restores a person's freshness, while recapitulate restores nothing — it revisits, point by point, to fix the whole in mind. And never let it shade into capitulate, which means to surrender.

    At seven each morning, the night nurses at St. Brigid's hand the ward to the day team. The handoff follows a strict script: the charge nurse must recapitulate the night for every patient in turn -- what changed, what was given, what to watch. Nothing may be skipped and nothing taken out of order, because the day team builds its plan from that summary alone. New nurses sometimes offer a quick highlight or two instead. They are stopped and sent back to the top of the list. The rule sounds rigid, but veterans defend it: a systematic retelling, point by point, keeps a small detail from slipping through at the moment shifts change.
