duress

/doo-RES/ - rhymes with press

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Duress means unlawful pressure or threats used to force someone to act.

See the word in context

Historians note that the border treaty was sealed under duress, with the enemy's army camped within sight of the palace walls.

Under duress from the smugglers, who had named his sister's school and her bus route, the harbor master left the east gate unlocked.

Duress is not ordinary stress or persuasion — it is pressure so improper, usually threats against a person, their family, or their property, that the law treats choices made under it as not truly chosen at all.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    coercion: Coercion is the act of forcing someone — what the threatener does, seen from the threatener's side. Duress is the same event seen from the victim's side: the unlawful pressure they were under, which is why the law says a signature given under duress does not bind.

    extortion: Extortion is the crime of squeezing money or favors out of someone by threats — it names what the criminal is after. Duress names the victim's condition: pressure so improper that the law treats whatever was agreed under it as never truly chosen.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    consent: agreement given freely, with no pressure attached

    volition: one's own free choice — the law's opposite of duress

    Where it sits · how far past lawful persuasion it goes

    pressure → intimidation → duress

    pressure vs. duress: Pressure is far milder than duress — deadlines, expectations, and insistent asking can all lean on you without any threat behind them.

    intimidation vs. duress: Intimidation is a shade weaker than duress — the fear is real, but duress is the legal term for pressure so improper that a signature given under it does not count.

      Section 3

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      formal / legal

      Duress lives in courtrooms and contracts, almost always in the phrase 'under duress.' Everyday pressure is just 'pressure'; reach for duress when the pressure crosses into threats and the choice stops being a real choice. It is the noun-side partner of its unit-mate coerce: the coercer applies the threats, and the victim acts under duress.
