retract

/rih-TRAKT/

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Retract means to withdraw a statement, promise, or claim, especially because it was found to be wrong.

See the word in context

The newspaper had to retract its front-page report once documents proved the local factory had actually passed its safety inspection.

After an outside team found a critical error in the lab's measurements, the chemist chose to retract the paper rather than defend a flawed result.

Retract fits because the chemist is formally taking back a published claim — the paper — specifically because it was discovered to be wrong, which is the word's core situation in science and publishing.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    withdraw: Withdraw means to pull back or take back something offered or stated — a bid, an application, a remark — and it is the general word for any pulling-back. Retract is the pointed version: you take back a specific public statement or claim because it proved wrong. You can withdraw an offer that was perfectly sound; a retraction admits error.

    rescind: Rescind means to officially cancel a decision, offer, or rule — a company rescinds a job offer, a board rescinds a policy. What gets rescinded is an act with legal or official force. Retract applies to things said or published: statements, claims, and testimony taken back as mistaken.

    recant: Recant means to formally give up a belief or position one once professed, classically under pressure — heretics were made to recant. Retract is narrower and factual: you take back a particular statement or claim because it turned out to be wrong, with no change of creed required.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    affirm: to state firmly that something is true

    maintain: to continue insisting that a claim is correct

    Where it sits · how fully you go back on it

    qualify → retract → abjure

    qualify vs. retract: To qualify a statement is weaker — you soften it or add conditions while still standing behind it, instead of retracting it outright.

    abjure vs. retract: To abjure is stronger — it is a solemn, often sworn renouncing of a belief entirely, going beyond retracting one mistaken statement.

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Neutral–formal

    Retract is the standard word in journalism, science, and law — newspapers retract stories, researchers retract papers, witnesses retract testimony. It also keeps its literal 'pull back' sense (a cat retracts its claws). In casual conversation, people usually say 'take back' instead.
