allusion

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Allusion means an indirect reference to a person, event, or work without naming it outright.

See the word in context

The op-ed never names the old story, but its closing line about sour grapes is an allusion most readers catch at once.

Accepting the award, the director thanked 'everyone who ever called me a scruffy-looking dreamer' — an allusion to the space saga she grew up on. She never named the film; she didn't need to. Half the room grinned, and the other half looked it up that night.

Allusion is more precise than 'reference' because it stays indirect: the source goes unnamed, and the line rewards listeners who already know it. Name the film outright, with title and year, and the allusion dissolves into ordinary citation.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    veiled reference: A veiled reference is any pointer kept deliberately indirect, and the veil can be thick — sometimes the writer half-hopes it slips past unnoticed. An allusion is the artful version: still unnamed, but tuned so that readers who know the source catch it at once and feel rewarded.

    knowing nod: A knowing nod is the quickest of gestures — a beat, a phrase, a raised eyebrow toward something the audience must recognize on its own. An allusion does the same pointing in words, and it can carry real weight in a text rather than staying a passing wink.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    outright naming: calling the source by name, leaving nothing to catch

    explicit citation: the source stated in full — title, author, date

    Where it sits · how openly the source is invoked

    echo → allusion → direct quote

    echo vs. allusion: An echo is fainter than an allusion — a turn of phrase that merely recalls another work, perhaps without the writer even intending it.

    direct quote vs. allusion: A direct quote goes further than an allusion: it reproduces the exact words, and usually names where they came from.

      Word Choice

      literary / neutral

      Use allusion for an indirect, unnamed pointer the audience must catch on its own. If the source is named, call it a citation or a quotation; and if a whole work sustains a hidden meaning rather than touching it once, you are describing an allegory.

Her final line puzzled a few readers: 'We'll always have the courtyard.' By Monday someone had taped a movie poster above the coffee maker, proof enough that the line had landed.
