Meaning

What It Means

Concomitant means naturally accompanying something else.

See the word in context

Every gold rush brings its concomitant swarm of outfitters, claim lawyers, and saloon keepers.

When the reservoir was drawn down for dam repairs, the engineers published the schedule a year ahead: eight feet of water gone by June. What the notices never mentioned were the drop's concomitant changes — reed beds stranded dry, the herons gone by July, a swimming beach turned to mudflat. None of it was the point of the project; all of it arrived with the project.

Concomitant is the exact word for those changes, because concomitant means naturally accompanying something else — the mudflat came with the drawdown the way a shadow comes with a lamp. And note what the word does not say: a concomitant thing travels alongside; it is not the cause of its partner, and calling it the result would already be claiming more than the word claims.

    Accuracy

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    attendant: Attendant is the formal twin, found in the same reports and rulings as concomitant. Attendant circumstances belong to the main event's own package, expected whenever it occurs; concomitant leans harder on the traveling itself — the effect arrives alongside its partner, reliably, rather than waiting upon it like staff.

    accompanying: Accompanying is the plain, everyday word for whatever comes along with something else, even a single time. Concomitant makes the stronger claim that the two things pair naturally and reliably — the second arrives with the first every time, closer to a shadow than a guest.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    unrelated: sharing nothing with the main event but a calendar

    standalone: occurring entirely on its own

    Where it sits · how reliably the two things pair

    occasional → concomitant → inseparable

    occasional vs. concomitant: Occasional is a far looser pairing than concomitant — an occasional companion turns up alongside now and then, on no schedule, while a concomitant one arrives with its partner every time.

    inseparable vs. concomitant: Inseparable goes further than concomitant — a concomitant effect reliably comes along; inseparable things cannot be pried apart at all.

      Section 3

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      formal / academic

      Concomitant belongs to reports, medicine, and economics — the register where effects ride along with policies. It is not a causal word: a boom's concomitant noise accompanies the boom, and whether one causes the other is a separate claim the word declines to make. Attendant is its formal twin; in conversation, say 'that comes with it.'

The surgeons' notes tracked the healing; the physical therapists tracked everything that healing dragged along with it. Muscle loss proved to be the concomitant the therapists could set their watches by: every chart in the study showed measurable thinning of the leg muscles by the tenth day of rest, without a single exception. Other effects came and went — some patients slept badly, a few lost appetite — but the muscle loss arrived with the bed rest as reliably as a shadow arrives with a lamp. The study's recommendation was blunt: if the rest is prescribed, the strength work must be prescribed beside it.
