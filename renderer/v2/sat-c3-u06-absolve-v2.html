Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Absolve means to formally clear someone of guilt or responsibility.

See the word in context

The colliery inquest absolved the shift foreman of blame for the collapse, finding he had ordered the gallery cleared a full hour before the props gave way.

When the orphanage's winter fund came up short, whispers followed the matron for months; the trustees' review absolved her completely, tracing every missing shilling to a bank failure and none to her hands.

Absolve is broader than a courtroom word — a panel can absolve you of guilt, but you can also be absolved of a duty, a debt, or a promise. It carries a formal, almost ceremonial weight: an authority declares the burden lifted.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    pardon: A pardon forgives a proven offense: the guilt stays on the record, but the punishment is set aside as an act of mercy. Absolve goes further — it declares the guilt or the duty itself lifted, so there is nothing left on the books to punish. A pardon spares; absolution erases.

    acquit: Acquit is the jury's word: a court finds the charge unproven at trial and the defendant walks free. Absolve is broader and more ceremonial — a priest, a panel, or a court can absolve, and it can lift duties, debts, and promises as well as guilt.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    blame: to fix the fault on someone

    punish: to make them pay for it

    Where it sits · how officially the guilt is lifted

    excuse → forgive → absolve

    excuse vs. absolve: A teacher may excuse a missed lesson and think no more of it; excuse stops far short of absolve, which formally declares a person free of the charge.

    forgive vs. absolve: To forgive is warm and personal, and the fault may still stand on the books; forgive stops short of absolve, which wipes the responsibility away with official weight.

      In the hungry winter of 1797, the parish of Greenhollow faced a ledger problem with a human face: forty war widows owed the common granary for seed their late husbands had borrowed. The law allowed the parish to collect from the households, and a few councilmen argued it must. Instead, at a meeting recorded in the vestry book, the parish voted to absolve the widows of the whole debt, striking every name from the granary rolls. The clerk noted that the vote was not charity in the usual sense, for the debts were real and lawfully owed. But once the entry was made, nothing remained on the books to collect: the obligation itself had been erased, not merely postponed or reduced.
