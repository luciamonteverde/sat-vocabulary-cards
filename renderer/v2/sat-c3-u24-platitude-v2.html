What It Means

Platitude means a stale, unoriginal remark.

See the word in context

The fortune cookie offered its usual platitude — 'every journey begins with a single step' — which felt thin beside a dinner this good.

The graduation speaker had twelve minutes and used them on platitudes: follow your passion, reach for the stars, be true to yourself. The valedictorian, given three, told one true story about the school's janitor — and hers was the speech parents quoted at dinner.

A platitude is a remark worn featureless by use: once wise, now automatic, offered where thinking should go. It differs from a proverb, which stays useful, and from small talk, which never claimed depth — the platitude poses as wisdom while delivering none.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    bromide: A bromide is a soothing, stale saying handed out like a pill — meant to calm the moment rather than to enlighten it. A platitude poses as wisdom; a bromide only means to comfort. Both are worn out, but the platitude pretends to depth while the bromide pats your hand.

    proverb: A proverb is a worn saying that still earns its keep — 'measure twice, cut once' is old and still saves lumber. A platitude is a saying that has stopped working: worn smooth, posing as wisdom, delivering none. Age ripens a proverb; it only wears a platitude smoother.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    insight: a fresh observation that actually opens something up

    epigram: a polished, witty saying that still surprises

    Where it sits · how worn the saying has become

    remark → commonplace → platitude

    remark vs. platitude: A remark is neutral ground — maybe fresh, maybe dull; it stops well short of platitude on the staleness scale.

    commonplace vs. platitude: A commonplace goes further than a plain remark toward wear — everyone has heard it before — but a platitude completes the slide: worn smooth, posing as wisdom, saying nothing.

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      dismissive / critical

      Platitude is an accusation: the remark is stale and pretending to be wise. Bromide is gentler, commonplace gentler still; a cliche can be any overused phrase, not just mock-wisdom. Praise no one's platitudes — if the saying still earns its keep, call it a proverb or an adage instead.
