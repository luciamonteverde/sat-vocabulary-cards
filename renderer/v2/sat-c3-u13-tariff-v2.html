Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Tariff means a tax a government charges on goods as they cross its border — most often on imports, collected before the goods can enter the market.

See the word in context

The price tag never says tariff, but a fifth of what the toy costs was collected at the dock before it ever reached the shelf.

For its first decades the young republic ran almost entirely on the tariff: clerks at every customs house weighed, counted, and taxed incoming cargo, and those collections paid for the navy, the postal roads, and the president's salary.

Note where the tariff lives: at the border, on goods, at the moment of crossing. It is not a sales tax at the register, not a toll on travelers, and not a fine — it is trade's entry fee, and governments have used it both to raise money and to tilt the market toward home producers.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    duty: a tax on particular goods, most often collected at the border — in trade, tariff's everyday twin. Tariff is the wider, more official word: the whole published schedule of such charges, the one treaties and economists argue over.

    levy: any charge a government imposes by law — on income, land, or trade alike. A tariff is a levy of one specific kind: it stands at the border and taxes goods as they cross.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    subsidy: money a government pays to producers to support them — a boost rather than a charge

    exemption: an official release from a charge that others must pay

    Where it sits · how hard the state's hand comes down on foreign trade

    surcharge → tariff → embargo

    surcharge vs. tariff: A surcharge is lighter than a tariff: a modest extra fee tacked onto a bill almost apologetically, while a tariff is a standing border tax weighty enough to redraw what a country buys.

    embargo vs. tariff: An embargo goes far beyond a tariff: a tariff taxes foreign goods but lets them keep flowing, while an embargo cuts the trade off entirely — nothing crosses at any price.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    economic / journalistic

    Tariff is a border word: it taxes goods, at the crossing, under a published schedule. Inside the economy, other names take over — a sales tax at the register, a toll on the bridge, a fee for the permit, a fine for the violation. And mind the direction of the money: a tariff takes from imports; a subsidy gives to producers. Newspapers pair the two constantly because they are rival tools for the same goal.

    Rosa's bike shop posts its prices in chalk, and this spring every number on the board went up. The reason sits at the harbor: a new 30 percent tariff on imported bicycles, collected at the dock before a single crate reaches her stockroom. Rosa pays the charge to the customs office, then folds it into her chalk prices, which is how a border tax ends up in a shopper's receipt. Lawmakers say the charge will push buyers toward the two factories upstate that still make frames. Rosa is not so sure; she has watched customers simply keep their old bikes another year.
