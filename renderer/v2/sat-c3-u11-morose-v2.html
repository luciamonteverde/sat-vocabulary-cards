morose

/muh-ROHS/ - rhymes with dose

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Morose means sunk in gloom that has settled in and pushes company away.

See the word in context

After the bakery turned down his sourdough, Amos was morose for a week, kneading in silence and glaring at the proofing loaves.

Nobody could tease the old ferryman out of his morose spells: whole crossings passed in sighs and one-word answers, the passengers' jokes sinking into the grey water behind the boat.

Morose is deeper than a bad mood: settled gloom with an unsociable edge that pushes company away. A glum person is quiet for an afternoon; a morose one has let the darkness move in and answer the door.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    sullen: Sullen is silent resentment: the slammed door, the one-word answers aimed at whoever caused the mood. Morose shares the gloom but drops the grudge — a morose person is sunk in darkness that has settled in for weeks, not punishing anyone in particular.

    melancholy: Melancholy means gently, thoughtfully sad — wistful autumn evenings, old letters, a mood a person can almost enjoy. Morose strips away that sweetness: the gloom turns heavy and unsociable, with a sullen edge that pushes company away rather than inviting quiet reflection.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    jovial: heartily, laughingly good-humored

    chipper: briskly cheerful and energetic

    Where it sits · how deeply the low spirits settle in

    glum → morose → despairing

    glum vs. morose: Glum is milder than morose — a passing afternoon of low spirits, while a morose mood sinks into gloom that settles in and turns sullen enough to push company away.

    despairing vs. morose: Despairing goes further than morose — beyond gloom into the conviction that nothing will ever improve.

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    neutral to literary

    Morose is settled gloom that pushes company away — days or weeks of it, not a passing cloud. 'Glum' covers an afternoon; morose moves in and unpacks. And where its unit-mate dour describes only the stern, unsmiling surface, morose reports the weather inside: genuinely dark. A dour face can hide contentment; a morose one never does.

    When Uncle Rodrigo sold the restaurant, everyone expected a few quiet weeks. Instead he turned morose. The gloom arrived in June and simply stayed: curtains half drawn, calls unreturned, the chessboard on the porch untouched. He wasn't angry with the buyers, and he said so; he wasn't angry with anyone. The darkness had no address. By September, neighbors who dropped off soup had stopped ringing the bell. He would wave them off from the window, not unkindly, and go back to his chair. It was his sister who finally named the difference: sadness visits, she said, but this had moved in — heavy, unsocial, and in no hurry to leave.
