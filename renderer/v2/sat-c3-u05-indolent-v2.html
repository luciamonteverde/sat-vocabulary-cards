indolent

/IN-duh-luhnt/ - the stress falls on IN

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Indolent means habitually lazy; avoiding effort.

See the word in context

By the second week of the heat wave the whole office had turned indolent, meetings drifting past their own agendas.

My roommate Dev is brilliant and indolent in equal measure: he microwaves dinner in the same bowl he'll eat from, purely to spare himself the washing of one dish.

Indolent is a settled disposition — effort avoided on principle — not a mood. Listless people lack energy from illness or gloom; weary people have spent theirs. The indolent are fine; they simply decline the work. Latin in- ('not') + dolere ('to feel pain'): the word slid from painless to comfortable to work-shy.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    idle: Idle describes a state, not a character: the machine, or the worker, is simply not working right now, for any reason at all. Indolent is a settled trait — effort avoided as a way of life. A diligent person can be idle on a Sunday; only a habit makes someone indolent.

    sluggish: Sluggish blames low energy: the body or system is moving slowly because there is little power behind it. The indolent have energy to spare and simply decline to spend it on work. A sluggish river cannot hurry; an indolent roommate could wash the dish and won't.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    industrious: steadily hardworking by habit

    energetic: full of vigor and eager to spend it

    Where it sits · how far ease slides into vice

    unhurried → indolent → slothful

    unhurried vs. indolent: Unhurried is no vice at all — simply taking one's time — and it stops well short of indolent, which avoids the effort itself.

    slothful vs. indolent: Slothful goes further than indolent, turning a lazy habit into something closer to a settled vice.

      Apply

      Word Choice

      formal / literary

      Indolent paints laziness as a settled trait, with a faintly amused, literary tone — an indolent cat, an indolent August. Lazy is blunter; listless and sluggish blame energy, not character. Doctors keep the old painless sense: an indolent condition advances slowly and quietly.

      Every allotment garden has its legend, and on the Marsh Lane plots the legend was Desmond. Desmond was not weak and not tired. He biked eleven miles to the plots each Saturday, played two hard sets of badminton by the gate, and then settled into a folding chair beside a bed of weeds he had no intention of pulling. The committee called him indolent, and the word was chosen with care: his neighbors worked; Desmond supervised his own comfort. Offers of help embarrassed him into half an hour of hoeing, after which the chair reclaimed him. When the plot inspection came, Desmond charmed the inspectors so thoroughly that they left believing the thistles were a pollinator project.
