Meaning

Hear It

What It Means

Anachronism means something placed in the wrong time period.

See the word in context

Players spotted the anachronism within hours of the medieval game's release: a potato roasting on a tavern spit, four centuries before potatoes reached Europe.

Mr. Okafor ends every term the same way: he projects a painting of a 1776 treaty signing into which he has quietly edited one anachronism — a ballpoint pen resting on the desk — and offers extra credit to the first student who finds it. Most classes need twenty minutes. The pen looks perfectly ordinary, which is his point.

Anachronism is the precise word because the pen is not wrong in itself — it is wrong for the date. An anachronism is something placed in the wrong time period; the flaw lies in the placement, not the object. A ballpoint pen on a desk in 1976 is furniture; the same pen in 1776 is a time traveler.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    anomaly: An anomaly is anything that breaks the expected pattern — a warm day in January, a left-handed desk in a row of right-handed ones. An anachronism is one specific kind of anomaly: the pattern broken is time, and the thing sits in the wrong century.

    holdover: A holdover is a leftover that simply stayed on after its era ended — the fax machine still humming in one office. It got old in place. An anachronism, by contrast, has been put where it never belonged: the fault is placement, not lingering.

    relic: A relic is an old survivor prized simply for its age — a genuine piece of the past that reached the present honestly. Calling something an anachronism is a complaint, not a compliment: it says the thing sits in the wrong period, whether or not it is old at all.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    novelty: a thing new to its own moment

    period detail: a detail that belongs exactly to its era

    Where it sits · how far from its own time

    oddity → throwback → anachronism

    oddity vs. anachronism: An oddity is far milder than an anachronism — merely unusual, like a green door in a row of red ones; it says nothing about time, while an anachronism is odd in exactly one way: it sits in the wrong period.

    throwback vs. anachronism: A throwback stops short of an anachronism: a throwback is old-style by choice and still at home in its own day, while an anachronism belongs to a different period entirely.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    formal / critical

    Anachronism is the historian's and critic's noun for a thing in the wrong period — the complaint is about placement, not age, and the misplaced thing can even be too modern for its setting. Keep this unit's lanes straight: antiquated equipment is old and outdated but sits in its own present; antediluvian teases something for sheer comic age; an anachronism has wandered out of its century altogether.

Then a conservation student ran a routine pigment analysis and found Prussian blue in the waves. Prussian blue is a laboratory accident of 1704; no painter alive in 1480 could have owned a gram of it. The pigment was an anachronism -- a material sitting two centuries before its own existence -- and one out-of-time detail unraveled the whole attribution. The panel now hangs in the study collection, relabeled: a skilled nineteenth-century imitation, interesting for entirely different reasons.
