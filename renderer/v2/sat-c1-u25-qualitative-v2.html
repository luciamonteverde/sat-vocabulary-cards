qualitative

/KWAH-lih-tay-tiv/

Meaning

Hear It

What It Means

Qualitative means concerned with the kind, character, or qualities of something rather than with its amount — describing what a thing is like instead of counting how much of it there is.

See the word in context

The review board asked for qualitative evidence as well — patient interviews and case notes, not just the recovery statistics.

When the city studied its new crosstown bus route, the first team came back with numbers: ridership up 12 percent, average wait down 90 seconds. The second team rode the buses for a month and came back with something else — riders who said the route finally made evening shifts possible, drivers describing a tense merge near the depot, one student's account of homework finished on the long quiet stretch. The council called this second report the qualitative one, and read it twice.

The second team's findings measure nothing; they capture the character of the route — what kind of experience it creates, and for whom. Evidence about kind and character rather than quantity is exactly what qualitative describes.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    descriptive: Descriptive means setting down in words what something is like — a police sketch and a travel diary are both descriptive. Qualitative makes a further claim about method: it studies kind and character as its evidence, deliberately set against counting. All qualitative research is descriptive; most description is not research.

    interpretive: Interpretive means drawing out what evidence means — adding the layer of judgment that says why the details matter. Qualitative names the kind of evidence itself: interviews, observations, accounts of character. A study gathers qualitative material first; the interpretive work comes when someone argues what it shows.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    quantitative: based on amounts, counts, and measurements

    statistical: expressed through numerical data

    Where it sits · how far beyond plain description the analysis goes

    descriptive → qualitative → interpretive

    descriptive vs. qualitative: Descriptive is weaker than qualitative: a descriptive report simply sets down what was seen, while a qualitative study goes further, sorting those observations into kinds and drawing out their character.

    interpretive vs. qualitative: Interpretive goes further than qualitative: qualitative work classifies experience into kinds, while interpretive work pushes deeper still, arguing what those kinds finally mean.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    neutral, academic; the working vocabulary of research and analysis

    Qualitative is a claim about kind, not a compliment: qualitative data is not better data, and a 'qualitative croissant' is nonsense — the everyday word for excellence is high-quality. Keep it apart from anecdotal, which offers scattered personal stories without system; qualitative study is methodical about character, with sampling and sorting behind it. And watch the near-twin qualified, which means hedged or credentialed — a qualified researcher may do qualitative work, but the words say different things.

    When the city rebuilt Alder Plaza, the parks department reported the numbers first: gate counters logged four thousand visitors a week, nearly double the old plaza's traffic. But the council wanted to know what those visits were actually like, so it commissioned a qualitative study to run alongside the counts. For a month, two researchers sat on the benches with notebooks, recording how skateboarders and chess players shared the same corner and how parents clustered wherever the fountain spray reached. Their report sorted the plaza's life into kinds — morning regulars, lunchtime drifters, evening families — and quoted visitors on what the place felt like in their own words. Not one line of it could go in a spreadsheet, the lead researcher admitted, and that was the point: the study asked what kind of place the plaza had become, not how much it was used.
