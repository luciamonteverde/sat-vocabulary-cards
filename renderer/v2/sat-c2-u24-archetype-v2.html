Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

An archetype is the original, recurring pattern of a type — the model that later examples echo and are measured against.

See the word in context

The wily servant who outwits his master is an archetype: Roman comedies staged him, and sitcoms still cast him every fall.

Film students groan when the gray-bearded mentor dies at the end of act two, but that guide is an archetype older than film itself: medieval romance robed him as a wizard, westerns pinned a badge on him, and next summer's blockbuster has already cast him again.

Archetype is the precise word because the mentor is not one character but a pattern beneath characters — each era pours its own costume over the same figure. A single beloved mentor would be an example; the archetype is the mold that all such examples keep filling.

    Accuracy

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    exemplar: An exemplar is one outstanding case held up for imitation — admirable by definition. An archetype needn't be admirable at all: the tyrant and the trickster are archetypes too. The exemplar is a case; the archetype is the pattern underneath the cases.

    template: A template is a ready-made pattern you copy exactly, and it belongs to whoever drew it. An archetype is looser and older — a deep recurring figure that each new version reinvents without anyone handing out instructions.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    one-off: a single case that follows no pattern and starts none

    departure: a deliberate swerve away from the established pattern

    Where it sits · how much pattern-setting force the example carries

    instance → model → archetype

    instance vs. archetype: An instance is the weakest rung: any single case counts as an instance, while an archetype is the founding pattern that makes every later instance recognizable in the first place.

    model vs. archetype: Model sits below archetype: a model is a pattern worth copying here and now, while an archetype has set its pattern so deeply and for so long that new versions echo it without anyone deciding to copy.

      Section 3

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      literary / analytic

      Archetype belongs to criticism and psychology: reach for it when a figure or story-shape recurs across works, eras, or cultures — the flood story, the trickster, the reluctant hero. It is neutral about merit, unlike exemplar, which praises. And keep it clear of stereotype, the lazy social shortcut applied to real people; an archetype is a deep pattern in how stories get told.

      Professor Yusuf Adeyemi opens his mythology survey the same way every fall: with a farm boy who does not want to go. He shows his students a Sumerian shepherd, a Greek plowman, a medieval swineherd, and a moisture farmer from a 1977 space film, each summoned from his fields by a stranger bearing news. No storyteller in that chain read all the others, Adeyemi points out. Most read none of them. That, he tells the class, is what marks an archetype: a figure old and deep enough that new tellers reinvent it unprompted, and audiences recognize it before a word of explanation. The final assignment asks each student to find the reluctant farm boy in a story published in the last five years. In ten years of teaching, no student has ever failed to find one.
