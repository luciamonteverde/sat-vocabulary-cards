What It Means

Laconic means using very few words.

See the word in context

The lighthouse keeper's radio checks were famously laconic — 'Lamp lit. Sea up.' — and the coast guard learned to read whole storms into a third word.

Old Mr. Amos ran the hardware store on eleven words a day: a nod for hello, 'aisle three' for most questions, and — for a flooded basement, a cracked foundation, or a wedding — the same laconic verdict: 'Fixable.'

Laconic is more precise than 'quiet' or 'shy' because it describes how someone uses words, not whether they dare to speak: a laconic answer is complete, confident, and two words long. The quiet may be saying nothing; the laconic have said exactly enough.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    clipped: Clipped means short in a brisk, cool way — the scissors are audible, and the chill is part of the message. Laconic keeps the brevity but drops the frost: a laconic answer is composed, complete, and often admired, even warm. Clipped cuts you short; laconic simply doesn't waste words.

    sparing: Sparing means using little of anything it touches — money, praise, words — with a feel of rationing, and a sparing answer often gives less than the moment asks for. Laconic is different: a laconic reply is tiny yet complete, exactly enough said, with nothing the listener still needs held back.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    talkative: happy to keep the words coming

    long-winded: takes the scenic route through every sentence

    Where it sits · how tightly the message is compressed

    brief → laconic → silent

    brief vs. laconic: A brief answer is simply short — milder than laconic, which compresses so far that two words must carry the whole verdict.

    silent vs. laconic: Silent goes further than laconic: the laconic speaker still hands you a word or two, while silence hands you nothing at all.

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      admiring / dry

      Laconic usually carries quiet admiration — the two-word cowboy, the one-line telegram. For rudeness in brevity, English keeps 'curt'; for the trait of disliking talk altogether, 'taciturn.' Against its unit-mates: laconic speech is the exact opposite of bombastic's inflation, and unlike prosaic it says nothing about imagination — a laconic line can be the wittiest in the room.

      My grandmother crossed three continents in her seventies and reported the whole journey on postcards that averaged four words apiece. 'Alps. Bigger than advertised.' 'Cairo hot. Camel unimpressed.' The family called the style laconic; the post office probably called it a rounding error. The strange thing was how much arrived in so little. Four words told you the weather, the mood, and whether the camel ride had been worth it. When she was tired, you knew from the handwriting, never from extra words — there were never extra words. Her last card, from Lisbon, said only, 'Home Tuesday. Learn to make coffee.' We understood it completely, and we did.
