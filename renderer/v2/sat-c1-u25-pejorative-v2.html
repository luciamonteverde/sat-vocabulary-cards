pejorative

/pih-JOR-uh-tiv/

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Pejorative means expressing contempt or disapproval; describing a term that carries a negative, insulting sense.

See the word in context

The style guide warns reporters that 'bureaucrat' can read as pejorative and suggests 'civil servant' in neutral copy.

The astronomy club's newsletter once referred to amateur stargazers as 'dabblers.' Letters poured in: members who spent freezing nights logging variable stars did not consider themselves casual toy-scientists. The editor had meant it affectionately, but she recognized the problem — whatever her intent, the word landed as a put-down. The next issue replaced it with 'independent observers' and carried an apology for the pejorative label.

'Dabblers' lands as a belittling put-down regardless of the editor's intent — a term carrying that built-in disapproval is what pejorative describes.

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    derogatory: Derogatory describes any remark, gesture, or tone that shows a low opinion of its target — an aimed put-down in the moment. Pejorative is a property of the word itself: a pejorative term carries its sneer built in, so that merely using it signals contempt. A comment is derogatory; a label is pejorative.

    belittling: Belittling means making someone or something seem small or unimportant, usually through dismissive mockery that stops short of outright assault. A pejorative term does that same work in a single word — the label itself belittles its subject, whatever sentence it sits in.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    complimentary: expressing praise

    respectful: showing due regard

    Where it sits · how much contempt the wording carries

    unflattering → pejorative → abusive

    unflattering vs. pejorative: Unflattering is weaker than pejorative: an unflattering description is merely not kind, while a pejorative one carries built-in contempt.

    abusive vs. pejorative: Abusive is stronger than pejorative — abusive language attacks a person outright, while a pejorative term belittles more quietly, sometimes in a single word.

      When the natural history museum began revising its oldest galleries, the curators started with the labels, not the fossils. Placards from the 1950s described certain tools as the work of 'primitive' peoples, and that one word stopped the revision committee cold. The committee's linguist argued that 'primitive' had become pejorative: whatever the writer intends, the label itself now carries a sneer, ranking whole cultures as lesser. A curator objected that the old placards were meant as neutral chronology, not contempt. The linguist agreed about the intent — and answered that intent no longer travels with the word. The new labels say 'early' instead. Visitors, the committee reasoned, should be free to judge the tools without a term that has already judged the makers.
