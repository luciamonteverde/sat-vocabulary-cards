Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Allege means to assert something, usually without proof.

See the word in context

Neighbors allege that the factory drains its tanks after dark, but the inspectors' meters have yet to catch it.

For thirty years, retired deckhands alleged that the ferry company kept a second, cleaner maintenance log for the inspectors. The story surfaced in bar talk and letters to the editor, always vivid, never accompanied by so much as a photocopied page.

Allege is the precise verb because it means to assert something, usually without proof — the deckhands are not hinting or joking; they are stating a serious charge that still lacks its evidence. Claim comes close, but a claim can be about anything; allege carries the smell of accusation and the courtroom's 'not yet proven.'

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    assert: To assert is to state something firmly and confidently, standing behind it in the open — but with no charge of wrongdoing built in. To allege is to accuse: it points at misconduct while conceding the evidence is not in yet. You assert your own innocence; outsiders allege someone else's guilt.

    contend: To contend is to argue a position — a debater or scientist contends that a view is right, expecting pushback. To allege is narrower and darker: it lays a charge of wrongdoing while the proof is still missing. You contend in an argument; you allege in an accusation.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    prove: settles the matter with evidence

    deny: rejects the charge outright

    Where it sits · how much accusation the statement carries

    hint → claim → allege

    hint vs. allege: A hint sits far below allege — with a hint, the thing itself never even gets said out loud, while to allege is to state the charge openly and on the record.

    claim vs. allege: A claim stops short of allege — it puts an idea forward plainly, but without the weight of an accusation now waiting on proof.

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      formal / journalistic

      Allege is the reporter's and lawyer's verb: it lays a charge on the record while admitting the proof isn't in hand. Where this unit's assert states a position firmly and suggest merely floats one, allege accuses — reach for it only when wrongdoing is claimed and evidence is still pending.

      The trouble began with a single sentence in a food column: readers, the critic wrote, deserved to know what was really in Harborview's famous chowder. In the paragraphs that followed she went further, alleging that the restaurant's 'day-boat clams' arrived frozen, by truck, from two states away. The verb mattered. Her editor struck the word 'proved' from the draft and let 'allege' stand, because the paper had suppliers' gossip but no invoices. The restaurant denied everything and demanded a retraction. The critic kept her notes and waited. Three weeks later a delivery driver sold his route records to a rival paper. Every carton on them was frozen. What had been an accusation without proof became, overnight, a documented fact.
