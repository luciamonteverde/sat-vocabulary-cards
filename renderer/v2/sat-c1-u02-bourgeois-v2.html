Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Bourgeois means a middle-class person.

See the word in context

In the port city's old quarter, the tall brick houses were built by the bourgeois of the spice trade — merchants rich enough for glass windows but never welcome at court.

Digging through parish records, Lena finally placed her ancestor: not a noble, not a farmhand, but a bourgeois — a rope-maker who owned his workshop, employed six journeymen, and paid for a pew near the front of the church.

Bourgeois pins down a rank that 'middle class' blurs: it names the town-dwelling person of property — merchant, master craftsman, lawyer — who stood above the laborers yet below the nobility, comfortable but untitled.

    Accuracy

    “Does this word fit the meaning exactly, or does a neighbour fit better?”

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    burgher: A burgher is the bourgeois's near-twin in the Dutch and German towns: a town citizen of means, with a workshop, warehouse, or trade behind his name. Same rank, different map — and where bourgeois later picked up a sneer, burgher has stayed a plain historical label.

    townsman: A townsman is anyone who lives in a town — the word records where he sleeps, not what he owns. A bourgeois is a particular kind of townsman: one with property and a trade, a shop or workshop of his own. Every bourgeois is a townsman; a penniless renter is only a townsman.

    merchant: A merchant lives by buying and selling — the word names his trade, not his rank. Bourgeois names the rank itself: the propertied town class a successful merchant belonged to, alongside master craftsmen and lawyers. A ruined merchant is still a merchant; he may no longer be a bourgeois.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    aristocrat: the titled noble the bourgeois stood beneath

    peasant: the rural laborer the bourgeois stood above

    Where it sits · rank in the old social order

    peasant → bourgeois → aristocrat

    peasant vs. bourgeois: The peasant stood on the old social ladder's lowest rung, working land he rarely owned; the bourgeois lived a full rung up, in town, with a shop or trade of his own.

    aristocrat vs. bourgeois: The aristocrat looked down on the bourgeois from the ladder's top: whatever fortune a bourgeois piled up, he could not buy the birth and title the highest rung required.

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      historical / sometimes scornful

      Use bourgeois for the middle-class townsperson of history — the merchant above the peasant and below the aristocrat, both of them rungs on this card's ladder. Be alert to its second life as an adjective of scorn ('how bourgeois!'), sneering at comfortable middle-class taste; as this unit teaches it, it is the noun for the person, and burgher is its friendlier historical twin.

      When historians opened the tax ledgers of Rouen for the 1780s, they found the city's money in unexpected hands. The great fortunes did not belong to the old landed families. They belonged to the bourgeois: printers, cloth dealers, and lawyers who owned their shops and houses outright. Such a man paid taxes on property, hired apprentices, and sat on parish boards, yet no invitation to the intendant's table ever came. The ledgers record the frustration almost by accident. One printer listed his assets, then added a note protesting that rank at court still followed birth alone. For all his houses and presses, the printer stood where his class always stood -- above the laborers he hired, below the nobles he could not join.
