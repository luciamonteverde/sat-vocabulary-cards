pernicious

/per-NISH-us/

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Pernicious means causing gradual, insidious, and serious harm.

See the word in context

Public-health officials called the old lead pipes a pernicious threat: the metal leached into the water so slowly that families drank it for years without suspecting harm.

The rumor started as a single post claiming the town's reservoir was unsafe. Nothing about it seemed urgent, and officials ignored it. Over the following year it spread quietly through group chats, and bit by bit people stopped trusting every water-quality report the town published. By the time engineers proved the reservoir was fine, the pernicious falsehood had done damage no press release could undo.

The rumor's harm was slow, quiet, and deep — spreading unnoticed for a year while undermining public trust — which is exactly the kind of damage pernicious describes.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    insidious: Insidious describes the method of harm: it advances stealthily, in steps too small to notice, often wearing a harmless face. Pernicious covers method and outcome together — harm that creeps in quietly and also proves deep and serious. Call a tactic insidious for how it sneaks; call it pernicious once the damage runs deep.

    malignant: Malignant means actively hostile in nature — in medicine, a tumor that invades and spreads; elsewhere, malice at work. Pernicious harm is just as serious but needs no villain: it creeps in quietly and does deep damage unnoticed. Malignant points to active malice or aggression; pernicious to slow, hidden ruin.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    harmless: causing no damage at all

    wholesome: good for health or well-being

    Where it sits · how deep the harm finally runs

    irritating → pernicious → deadly

    irritating vs. pernicious: An irritating habit merely annoys, while a pernicious one causes real and lasting harm.

    deadly vs. pernicious: Deadly goes beyond pernicious — it kills outright rather than harming slowly from within.

      Every exam season, the same advice circulates through the dorms: skip sleep, drink coffee, and cram. Learning researchers consider the advice pernicious. It never announces a cost up front; students who follow it even feel productive at two in the morning. But the damage compounds quietly. Sleep is when the brain consolidates memory, so each all-nighter erases part of the studying it was meant to rescue. By the third exam cycle, chronic crammers in one long-running study scored a full letter grade below classmates with steadier habits. The myth survives because its harm is slow and hidden; no single bad night is dramatic enough to take the blame.
