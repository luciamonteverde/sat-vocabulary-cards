Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Bequeath means to leave something to someone through a will.

See the word in context

The comedian bequeathed his notebooks to the colleague who had opened for him for thirty years, with instructions to steal only the best jokes.

Old Marta the blacksmith let her apprentice borrow the good anvil but never gave it away. The forge, the tongs, the anvil, and the little book of hammer notes stayed hers until her will was read; she had bequeathed all of it, exactly as she put it in writing, to the apprentice who had swept her floor for nine years.

Bequeath is the exact word for what Marta did, because it means to leave something to someone through a will — the handover waits for the will to be read. Hand down comes close, but a grandmother can hand down a ring on any birthday; bequeath keeps the lawyer, the paper, and the waiting in the picture.

    Accuracy

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    leave: Leave is the plain-speech verb a will uses — she left the house to her niece — and it works just as well for a coat left on a train. Bequeath is the formal, legal version: it only ever means a transfer written into a will, delivered after the giver's death.

    endow: To endow is to give an institution a permanent fund — a chair, a lecture series, a hospital wing — and a living giver can endow and then watch the money work. Bequeath moves a gift on paper, effective only at death: endowments are about ongoing income, a bequest about the final handover.

    donate: Donating is giving to a cause or charity — money, blood, canned goods — usually while the giver is alive to sign the form. Bequeath is personal and posthumous: property named in a will, passing to a chosen person or institution only at the giver's death.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    disinherit: strike someone out of the will

    keep back: hold on to what someone hoped to receive

    Where it sits · how much legal formality the transfer involves

    give → hand down → bequeath

    give vs. bequeath: Give is far less formal than bequeath — a sandwich or a pencil changes hands with no paperwork and no waiting, while a bequeathed gift needs a will, a signature, and a reading to arrive.

    hand down vs. bequeath: To hand something down goes further than to give it — a younger generation is implied — but it can happen at any birthday, while bequeath waits for the reading of a will.

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      formal / legal

      Bequeath belongs to wills: it is how a benefactor gives one final time, on paper, effective at death. For a ring passed across the dinner table, hand down is the honest word; keep bequeath for the lawyer's office. Largess and munificence, its unit-mates, describe giving the giver is alive to enjoy.
