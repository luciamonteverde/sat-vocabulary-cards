Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Requisition means a formal demand for goods or property made by an authority.

See the word in context

A requisition arrived by motorcycle at dawn: every ferry on the lake now answered to the army.

The letter that closed the school reads politely and offers no choices. By order of the district commander, the gymnasium and both classroom wings would receive two hundred convalescent soldiers on Friday; desks were to be stacked in the yard; the boiler would be kept lit at the army's expense. A receipt was enclosed. The headmistress filed it, as instructed, under R — for requisition.

Requisition is the exact word for that letter, because a requisition is a formal demand for goods or property made by an authority — the courtesies of a request with none of its freedom. A seizure would simply have taken the building; the requisition documents the taking, names a rate, and files the paperwork, which changes the tone but not the outcome.

    Accuracy

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    seizure: A seizure simply takes the thing — the trucks are gone, and no paper changes hands. A requisition is the documented demand: the authority names the goods, leaves a receipt, and often sets a rate. Both end with the state holding your property; only the requisition files the paperwork.

    levy: A levy is a demand measured out in money or men — a tax to pay, a quota of recruits to send, counted rather than named. A requisition points at the goods themselves: these ferries, that empty warehouse, the trucks in your yard. Authority demands both; they differ in what is counted.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    donation: handed over freely, unasked

    purchase: taken only at a price the owner agreed to

    Where it sits · how forcibly the goods are taken

    request → requisition → confiscation

    request vs. requisition: A request is far weaker than a requisition — it leaves the choice on your side of the counter, and no is a complete answer; a requisition wears the same polite manners and takes the choice away.

    confiscation vs. requisition: Confiscation goes further than requisition — a requisition takes your trucks against a receipt and a rate; confiscation takes them and calls them the state's.

      Section 3

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      formal / bureaucratic

      Requisition is supply-office language — armies, emergencies, quartermasters — and the paper matters: it is a demand wearing a form's clothing. A request can be refused; a levy is counted in money or conscripts; a seizure or confiscation drops the paperwork and just takes. In an office the word lives a tamer life: you requisition staplers, and the only casualty is a budget line.

      The earthquake left four hundred families sleeping in the stadium parking lot, and the county's shelters were full by the second night. On the third morning, the emergency office issued a requisition for the Hotel Miramar: ninety rooms, named floor by floor, at a rate fixed by the statute. The owner, Mr. Anand, read the order twice. It was not a suggestion, and it did not pretend to be — but it was signed, itemized, and it promised payment within sixty days. His lawyer confirmed what the paper said: he could bill the county, he could keep the receipt, and he could not say no.
