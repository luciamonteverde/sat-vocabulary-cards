What It Means

Abjure means to reject or renounce something formally and publicly.

See the word in context

As a condition of joining the neutral mapmakers' guild, the navigator had to abjure, in writing and before witnesses, her allegiance to the crown that had trained her.

When the duchy changed hands, every officer of the old garrison was marched to the square and made to abjure the deposed duke — each man reading the renunciation aloud with his hand on the charter.

Abjure is more exact than quit or reject because it names a renunciation that is formal and public, often sworn aloud — you abjure an oath, a belief, or an allegiance on the record, not quietly in your heart.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    recant: To recant is to take back a statement or belief you once voiced publicly, usually under pressure. Abjure is broader and more ceremonial: you can abjure a tie you never put into words, swearing it away formally before witnesses. Recant undoes your own past words; abjure casts the thing itself off.

    disavow: To disavow is to deny that something is yours — a statement, a policy, a follower's actions — refusing responsibility or connection. Abjure goes further: it is a sworn, public renunciation made on the record. Disavowing pushes a thing away by denying it; abjuring formally gives it up before witnesses.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    embrace: take something up warmly and openly

    affirm: declare publicly that you stand by it

    Where it sits · how much ceremony surrounds the no

    refuse → renounce → abjure

    refuse vs. abjure: Refuse is the everyday no — you can refuse seconds at dinner without a second thought; abjure is a renunciation made formally and in public.

    renounce vs. abjure: Renounce is milder only in its setting: you can renounce a habit privately, while abjure adds the ceremony — witnesses, a record, sometimes an oath.

    Word Choice

    formal / ceremonial

    Abjure is the most formal and public of this unit's giving-up words: you abjure an oath, belief, or allegiance in front of witnesses, often in a legal or ceremonial setting. Forsake is the emotional cousin — deserting something you love; eschew is a quiet standing habit of avoidance. For everyday refusals, plain 'reject' or 'give up' fits better.

    When the old smuggler applied for a harbor pilot's license, the maritime board set one condition beyond the usual exams. He would have to abjure the free-trading life in open session: stand before the assembled commissioners, speak a renunciation drafted by the clerk, and sign it into the board's permanent record. Friends told him a quiet promise should be enough -- he had not run a crate of contraband in nine years. The board disagreed. Private resolve, however sincere, was invisible; what the harbor needed was a renunciation made where everyone could see it, sworn and filed. So he did it, hat in hand, voice steady. Witnesses said afterward that the words changed nothing about the man and everything about what the town could now expect of him.
