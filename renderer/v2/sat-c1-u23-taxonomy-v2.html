Meaning

Hear It

What It Means

Taxonomy means the scientific practice of classifying organisms into ordered categories.

See the word in context

A single beetle collected in Borneo forced a complete revision of the family's taxonomy.

For ninety years a museum filed one small brown bat with the free-tailed bats, on the strength of its wing shape alone. Genetic sequencing in 2011 showed it was not closely related to them at all. The curators moved the specimen into a genus of its own, revised the labels on four drawers, and published the change so that other collections could follow. The bat itself never changed; only its place in the taxonomy did.

Every step described is a matter of sorting one organism into the correct nested category of genus, family, and order, which is exactly what taxonomy means.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    classification: Classification is the general act of arranging things into ordered groups, and anything can be classified -- books, clouds, wines. Taxonomy is classification of living things under biology's formal rank system, where every organism takes a place from kingdom down to species.

    cataloguing: Cataloguing means recording items one by one in an organized list, the way a registrar logs a museum's holdings. Taxonomy does more than list: it assigns each organism to nested, ranked categories, kingdom down through family to species, following biology's published rulebook.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    jumble: a disordered mass with no arrangement at all

    disarray: a state of complete disorder

    Where it sits · how formal and rule-bound the sorting is

    grouping → taxonomy → systematics

    grouping vs. taxonomy: Grouping is the everyday act of putting like with like, while taxonomy is grouping governed by formal ranks, rules, and published names.

    systematics vs. taxonomy: Systematics reaches further than taxonomy, taking in the evolutionary relationships behind the categories rather than just the categories themselves.

      Section 3

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      scientific and technical

      Taxonomy is about where an organism belongs, not about what it is called: the separate business of choosing and standardizing names is nomenclature. Use taxonomy for the ranked categories themselves, such as species, genus, and family, and for the practice of assigning organisms to them.

      When the first platypus skin reached London in 1799, the naturalist George Shaw checked it for stitches, suspecting a hoax. A furred animal with a duck's bill and a beaver's tail fit nowhere in the existing taxonomy. Every rank in the system demanded a decision: mammal or not, and if a mammal, of what order? The rules allowed no animal to sit outside the table. In the end the platypus forced the creation of a new order, the monotremes, with its own place from kingdom down to species. The strange animal did not break the system; it showed why the system needed room to grow.
