Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Immutable means not able to be changed.

See the word in context

Mathematics deals in immutable truths — no vote, fashion, or invention will ever make seven times eight anything but fifty-six.

Grandfather ran the barbershop under one immutable rule: cash only. Landlords changed, prices changed, the neighborhood changed its name — and customers who tried a card in 1975 got the same amused headshake customers got in 2005. Persuasion never had a chance; the rule was not merely unbroken, it was untouchable.

Immutable makes a harder claim than long-standing or strict: not able to be changed. A strict rule is enforced; a long-standing one happens to have survived. Calling the rule immutable says change itself was off the table — no process, price, or person could move it.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    unchangeable: Unchangeable makes the same hard claim in plainer dress: it cannot be altered. Immutable adds register and reach — it is the word for mathematical truths, physical constants, and rules with no rewrite process, where unchangeable can describe a stubborn timetable. Both words break if a committee could ever vote the thing different.

    permanent: Permanent promises duration — the ink, the record, the appointment will last from now on. It does not say change is impossible; a permanent policy can still be repealed, it is just that nobody expects to. Immutable makes the stronger claim: no power anywhere can change it at all.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    changeable: open to alteration at any time

    revisable: able to be formally revised or updated

    Where it sits · how far beyond anyone's power to alter

    settled → fixed → immutable

    settled vs. immutable: A settled question could always be reopened someday — settlement is a truce with change; immutable never signed one.

    fixed vs. immutable: Fixed means firmly set — but a fixed date can still be moved by whoever fixed it. Immutable removes the mover: nothing and no one can change it.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    formal / emphatic

    Immutable is an all-or-nothing word — save it for what truly cannot change: mathematical facts, physical constants, rules with no rewrite process. It describes what a thing is; its unit-mates irreversible and irrevocable describe what has been done — a change that can't run backward, a decision that can't be recalled. If mere effort or a committee could change it, the word is overstating.

    At the science museum, Ms. Ide runs a demonstration she calls Vote on Gravity. Fourth graders draft rules for a marble run — how many marbles, who goes first — and pass them by show of hands, amending freely as they go. Then she asks the class to vote that marbles roll uphill. The vote passes, twenty hands to none. The marbles keep rolling down. The lesson, she tells them, is that the run's rules were theirs to rewrite, but the law of gravity is immutable — no majority, however large, can move it. She ends by asking which other rules in their lives are votable, and which are beyond any vote. The homework lists are famously long.
