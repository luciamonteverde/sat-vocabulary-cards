What It Means

Elicit means to draw out or bring forth a response.

See the word in context

A well-timed pause on stage can elicit more laughter than the punch line itself.

The audiologist's booth game is rigged in the kindest way: a soft tone plays in one earpiece, and a lit-up dancing bear rewards the toddler for turning toward it. No instruction could get a two-year-old to report what she hears, so the test is built to elicit the head-turn instead.

Elicit is exact because the response is drawn out deliberately, by design, from someone who could not simply be asked. Evoke would suit feelings or memories drifting up on their own; extract would imply force; elicit names the skillful drawing-out of a response that was there to give.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    evoke: Evoke draws out feelings, memories, or images that come up on their own — an old photograph evokes a face, a certain slant of light evokes a winter. Elicit points at people and design: a response deliberately drawn out of someone, the answer or laugh a technique was built to produce.

    prompt: A prompt is the small cue that triggers a response — a sticky note, a stage whisper, one leading question. Elicit is the fuller act of drawing the response out, often skillfully and by design; the prompt is only the nudge that starts it.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    stifle: to choke a response off before it can appear

    squelch: to crush a response once it has started

    Where it sits · how much force pulls the answer out

    invite → elicit → extract

    invite vs. elicit: To invite a response is gentler than to elicit one — an invitation leaves the door open, while eliciting actively draws the response out.

    extract vs. elicit: Extract is stronger than elicit: what you elicit comes willingly once drawn, but what you extract is pulled out against resistance.

      Word Choice

      formal / neutral

      Elicit is for responses deliberately drawn out of someone — answers, laughter, a reflex. Mind the homophone: elicit is a verb meaning to draw out; illicit is an adjective meaning against the rules.

      The first thing Dr. Okafor checks on a newborn is nothing the baby knows how to hide. She strokes the sole of one tiny foot, and the toes fan out. She turns the head gently to the side, and the opposite arm stretches. None of this is conversation. The baby is not answering questions. Yet each move is designed to elicit a response the nervous system cannot help giving. That is the point: a startled cry could be temperament, and a smile could be gas, but a reflex drawn out on demand is information. If the technique is right, the response follows. Residents spend weeks learning the difference between poking a baby and examining one. The baby's part never changes; the skill is entirely in the drawing out.
