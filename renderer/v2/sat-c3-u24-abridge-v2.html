abridge

/uh-BRIJ/ - rhymes with bridge

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Abridge means to shorten a written work while keeping its main content.

See the word in context

The publisher asked a scholar to abridge the two-volume biography into a single paperback that keeps every era of the senator's life.

For the schools tour, the theater company abridged the four-hour history play to seventy-five minutes: every act survives, every battle still happens, but the crowd scenes and repeated speeches are gone.

Abridge fits better than plain 'cut' because it promises the whole work is still there in miniature — main plot, main argument, main characters — whereas cutting or trimming makes no such promise about what survives.

    Accuracy

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    condense: To condense is to pack the same content into far fewer words, the way a summary does — the result can be a brand-new, tighter text of any shape. Abridge keeps the work's own shape: chapters, structure, and proportions survive in miniature, only shorter.

    cut: To cut is the blunt, everyday verb for removing parts, and it makes no promise about what survives. Abridge makes exactly that promise: the shortened book or play is still the whole work, main plot and main argument intact.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    pad: to stuff extra material in just to fill space

    restore: to put back what was taken out — an unabridged edition restores every cut

    Where it sits · how much of the book gets cut

    prune → abridge → gut

    prune vs. abridge: Pruning nips a stray sentence here and a repetition there; it stops well short of abridge, which can carry off half the book at once.

    gut vs. abridge: To gut a text goes further than to prune it — gutting rips out whole chapters until only a husk is left, with none of the faithfulness an abridged edition promises.

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    neutral / publishing

    Abridge belongs to whole written works: a book, play or report shrunk while its content survives. Abbreviate — its sibling on this unit's shelf — works on single words and names. If you are merely packing ideas tighter, condense is the plainer choice.

    The publisher's 'Classics in an Afternoon' series has one promise on its cover: we abridge, we never rewrite. Its editors keep every chapter of the original novel, in order, at a tenth of the length. A reader who later meets the full book finds the same rooms in the same house, only larger. The series founder is strict about the difference: a summary tells you about a story, while a shortened edition lets you live inside it. Purists grumble, but librarians report that children who start with the slim editions come back asking for the whole thing.
