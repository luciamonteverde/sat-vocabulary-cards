prescriptive

/prih-SKRIP-tiv/

Section 1

Meaning

Hear It

What It Means

Prescriptive means laying down rules for how something should be done, rather than describing how it actually is.

See the word in context

The old manual is strictly prescriptive: it tells clerks exactly which stamp to use, in which corner, in which ink.

Two grammar books sat side by side in the library. The first recorded how speakers across the country actually use 'who' and 'whom,' with maps and recordings. The second announced, in boldface, that 'whom' must always follow a preposition and that violators write 'substandard English.' Students quickly learned the difference: the first book described; the second issued orders. Only the second was prescriptive.

The second book issues rules about how language must be used instead of recording how it is used — that rule-laying stance is what prescriptive means.

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    directive: Directive means issuing authoritative instructions to people who answer to you — a directive memo tells particular staff what to do. Prescriptive rules aim wider: they lay down how the thing itself should be done, binding anyone who attempts it, whether or not they answer to the author.

    regulatory: Regulatory means imposing binding rules backed by official authority — the territory of agencies, statutes, and licensing boards. Prescriptive is a stance, not an office: any manual, critic, or teacher who dictates how things should be done is prescriptive, with or without the power to enforce a word of it.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    descriptive: recording how things actually are

    permissive: allowing free choice

    Where it sits · how binding the guidance becomes

    advisory → prescriptive → dogmatic

    advisory vs. prescriptive: Advisory is weaker than prescriptive: advisory guidance suggests what you might do, while prescriptive rules tell you what you must do.

    dogmatic vs. prescriptive: Dogmatic is stronger than prescriptive — a dogmatic authority insists its rules are beyond question entirely, while a prescriptive one at least confines itself to issuing them.

      Word Choice

      formal; academic, linguistic, and policy contexts

      Prescriptive lives in permanent contrast with descriptive — rules for how things should be versus records of how they are. Within this unit, keep it apart from generative (actually originating new things) and instructive (teaching useful insight): a prescriptive manual commands, it does not create or merely inform.

      The usage panel met in the dictionary's back room to settle a fight that had simmered for months. Half the editors wanted the new edition to record how people actually speak: if millions say 'less books,' the entry should note it without comment. The other half pushed for a prescriptive line — an entry that lays down 'fewer books' as the rule and tells readers plainly which form they should use. The chief editor reminded both camps that the dictionary employs no inspectors and issues no fines. Its only power is the reader's trust. In the end each disputed entry got two sentences: the first reporting what speakers do, the second stating what careful writers should do — a description and a rule, side by side.
