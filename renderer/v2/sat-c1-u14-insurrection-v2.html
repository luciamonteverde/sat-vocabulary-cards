Meaning

Hear It

What It Means

Insurrection means a violent uprising against a government or civil authority.

See the word in context

The insurrection began when dockworkers seized the customs house, and it ended a week later when the navy shelled their barricades.

In the colony's sugar islands, laborers armed with cane knives marched on the governor's mansion and held the capital for three days before warships arrived to crush the insurrection.

This is not a complaint or a peaceful protest: armed people rise up violently to seize power from the colonial government. An organized, violent uprising against authority is precisely an insurrection.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    uprising: An uprising is any act of open revolt by a group against those in power, the umbrella word for the whole family. An insurrection is an uprising turned violent and organized: armed people moving against a government or civil authority, the word historians and prosecutors reach for.

    revolt: A revolt is a violent refusal to keep obeying, the moment a group throws off commands, whether against a captain, a company, or a council, and it can be brief and local. An insurrection is aimed at government or civil authority and implies organization behind the violence.

    rebellion: A rebellion is organized armed resistance to a government, and it can grind on for years as a campaign. An insurrection is the eruption itself, a violent uprising concentrated in days or weeks; a long rebellion may begin with a single insurrection.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    obedience: willingly following rules and orders

    submission: yielding to a stronger power without resistance

    Where it sits · how far toward overthrow it goes

    unrest → insurrection → revolution

    unrest vs. insurrection: Unrest means scattered anger and protest, but an insurrection is an organized, often armed attempt to overthrow authority.

    revolution vs. insurrection: An insurrection is a violent uprising, but a revolution goes further and actually replaces the entire government or social order.

      Section 3

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      Formal / historical and legal

      Appears mostly in history books, news reports, and legal documents, often as 'armed insurrection' or 'put down an insurrection.' It names the event of a violent uprising; the related word 'insurgent,' built on the same Latin root surgere ('to rise'), names a person who takes part in one, so the two are related but never interchangeable. A peaceful march or petition is a protest, not an insurrection.

      When armed marchers stormed the tax offices of Port Serrin in 1846, they held the waterfront for exactly nine days. Historians still call those nine days an insurrection, and they choose the word with care. The violence was organized -- captains, passwords, a printed list of demands -- and it was aimed squarely at the city's civil authority. But it was an eruption, not a campaign: no army was raised, no war followed, and by the tenth morning the courts were open again. The trials that autumn used the same word, because the law, like the historians, needed a term for organized violence against authority that flares and is put down.
