What It Means

Bewildered means completely confused and unsure what is happening.

See the word in context

The new cashier stood bewildered before the register's forty unlabeled buttons while the lunch line grew behind her.

Handed his late aunt's ledger of codes and cross-references, the executor sat bewildered for an hour, unable to tell the savings from the debts.

Bewildered goes beyond 'confused': it means so completely lost that you can't find even the first step out — the word itself pictures someone swallowed by a wilderness with no path.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    baffled: Baffled means defeated by a specific problem you can't crack — the crossword clue, the error message that resists every fix. Bewildered is wider and more total: not one stubborn puzzle but the whole situation dissolving, so you can't even tell what the first step would be.

    disoriented: Disoriented means having lost your bearings in space or time — which way is north, what day it is — while your thinking may stay perfectly clear. Bewildered is deeper: the confusion takes over everything, not just your sense of direction.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    clearheaded: thinking crisply, with nothing fogging you

    composed: calm and collected, whatever is happening

    Where it sits · how completely the confusion takes over

    puzzled → bewildered → dumbfounded

    puzzled vs. bewildered: Puzzled is gentler than bewildered — a puzzled person is still working the problem, one wrong guess at a time.

    dumbfounded vs. bewildered: Dumbfounded goes further than bewildered, past confusion into a shock so complete that speech itself gives out.

      In the summer of 1893, seventeen-year-old Anders Lund stepped off a wagon from his family's farm into the Chicago World's Fair. His letters home do not describe wonder first. They describe a boy utterly bewildered: forty thousand strangers moving in every direction, electric light erasing the difference between evening and noon, machines whose purpose he could not begin to name. He writes that he stood by a fountain for an hour, 'not lost, exactly' — he could see his hotel's flag from where he stood — 'but not able to say what a person ought to do first.' It was not any single exhibit that undid him; it was everything at once. By the third day the letters change: he picks one hall a morning, and the fair shrinks to something a farm boy can learn.
