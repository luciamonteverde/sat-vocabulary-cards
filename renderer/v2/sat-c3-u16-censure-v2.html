Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Censure means harsh, condemning criticism — severe disapproval, especially the formal kind delivered officially and entered on the record.

See the word in context

The novelist shrugged off praise but hoarded every line of censure, taping the harshest reviews above his desk where he could argue with them daily.

The council's vote of censure carried no fine and took nothing from the harbor commissioner but her standing — yet the formal reading of her misdeeds into the public record ended her career as surely as any penalty could have.

Notice what the censure is: not shouting, and not a punishment with teeth. It is disapproval itself made official — criticism given the full weight of procedure, voted on and written down where everyone can read it. Censure strips reputation rather than money or freedom, and that is exactly why it stings.

    Accuracy

    “Does this word fit the meaning exactly, or does a neighbour fit better?”

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    rebuke: A rebuke is sharp — disapproval with an edge, fired straight at its target — and anyone can deliver one: a friend can rebuke a friend mid-conversation, with no office behind it. Censure is disapproval made institutional: voted on, written down, carried out on the record.

    reprimand: A reprimand is an official scolding from someone in authority — a captain reprimands a lieutenant, by the book and down the chain of command, and it can stay between the two of them. Censure is graver and more public: it can fall on anyone, even a peer or a senator, and it is meant to be seen.

    condemnation: Condemnation is the strongest public denunciation — it declares a person or act beyond defense, and it can thunder from any pulpit or editorial page. Censure is narrower and more procedural: a body formally placing its disapproval on record, stopping short of casting the offender out.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    commendation: official praise, formally delivered and entered on the record

    endorsement: public backing of a person, act, or plan

    Where it sits · how grave and official the disapproval is

    rebuke → censure → condemnation

    rebuke vs. censure: A rebuke is milder than censure: a rebuke is a sharp telling-off that can happen in a hallway and end there, while censure makes the disapproval formal, public, and permanent.

    condemnation vs. censure: Condemnation goes further than censure: censure places grave disapproval on the record but leaves the offender in the room, while condemnation declares the person or act beyond defense entirely.

      Section 3

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      formal / parliamentary

      Censure lives where disapproval turns official: senates censure members, boards censure licensees, and the word doubles as a verb for that act. Two traps: it is not censorship — a censor cuts the text, while censure condemns the person — and it is not a material penalty, because the sting is reputational, on the record. Chastise (also in this unit) is the act of calling a fault out, loudly and in the moment; censure is what a body's disapproval becomes once it is voted on and written down.

      When the dean canceled the language program without consulting anyone, the faculty senate did something it had not done in forty years: it drafted a formal motion of censure. The debate ran late. Nobody proposed firing her; removal was never on the table, and several of her sharpest critics said so plainly. What they wanted was the record. The motion passed, was read aloud, and was entered into the senate's published proceedings. The dean kept her office and her title. But the vote followed her — cited in the campus paper, mentioned at every budget hearing since. One retiring professor put it best: 'We could not undo the decision. We could make sure the disapproval outlived the afternoon.'
