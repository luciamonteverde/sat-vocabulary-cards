Meaning

What It Means

Addendum means an extra section added at the end of a book or document.

See the word in context

When the state grant arrived after the budget was printed, the clerk issued a one-page addendum with the revised totals.

The playbill had gone to press before the understudy stepped in, so ushers tucked a printed addendum inside each cover naming the night's actual cast, with a line of apology from the director.

Addendum is the exact word because it means an extra section added at the end of a book or document — the playbill stands as printed, and the correction arrives as its own attached piece. A footnote would sit at the bottom of a page; an addendum is a whole late arrival, formally joined on.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    appendix: An appendix is reference material planned from the start — tables, maps, and source lists the author always meant to attach at the back. An addendum is the unplanned arrival: it patches in what surfaced only after the text was finished. Both sit at the end; only an addendum comes late.

    insert: An insert is a loose extra sheet tucked between a document's pages, never bound in as part of the work. An addendum is formally joined on and becomes part of the document itself. Shake the binder and the insert falls out; the addendum stays put.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    deletion: matter taken out rather than added on

    abridgment: the whole work trimmed shorter

    Where it sits · how substantial the extra material is

    margin note → postscript → addendum

    margin note vs. addendum: A margin note is far slighter than an addendum — a reader's pencil scribble beside one paragraph, meant mostly for oneself, where an addendum is a whole printed section formally joined to the end.

    postscript vs. addendum: A postscript stops short of an addendum — a line or two slipped in after the signature, while an addendum arrives as a full section built onto the end of the work.

      The first edition of 'Gulls of the Northern Coast' went to press in March, its plates painted, its index set, its back matter complete. In May, a birder photographed a black-tailed gull off the headlands — a species the guide's range maps said should not exist within a thousand miles. Reprinting the whole book would have cost more than the small press earned in a year. Pulping it was unthinkable. The publisher chose the old remedy: a four-page addendum, bound into every later printing behind the index, with the new sighting, a corrected map, and one apologetic footnote. Collectors now prize the awkward first printings, but working birders want the later copies — the ones whose ending grew to admit what the sea sent after the text was finished.
