What It Means

Synopsis means a brief overview of a longer work — especially one that walks through a story's plot from beginning to end in a few compact lines.

See the word in context

The streaming menu's two-sentence synopsis gave away just enough of the plot to make Dev press play.

Because the opera would be sung in Italian, the printed program included a one-page synopsis: Act I's secret wedding, Act II's forged letter, and the duel that ends Act III, each set down in a few plain lines so the audience could follow every scene as it unfolded.

A whole three-act story, walked through start to finish on a single page — a compact overview like that is exactly what a synopsis is.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    abstract: Abstract means the formal paragraph at the head of a scholarly paper, stating its question and findings for researchers deciding whether to read on. A synopsis is its narrative cousin: it walks through a story or work, and it lives on book jackets and programs rather than in journals.

    rundown: Rundown means a quick, informal run through the main facts, often delivered out loud — 'give me the rundown.' A synopsis is the tidier, written form, and it usually retells a story rather than reporting a situation.

    précis: Précis means a strict miniature of a text that preserves its structure and proportions, a classroom exercise in disciplined compression. A synopsis is looser: it retells the plot in its own way, keeping whatever makes the story clear.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    epic: a long, expansive telling in which every episode is given in full

    elaboration: a spelling-out that adds detail rather than stripping it away

    Where it sits · how far the retelling is compressed

    digest → synopsis → logline

    digest vs. synopsis: A digest is gentler than a synopsis: it condenses a work but keeps whole passages of its texture, while a synopsis strips the story down to its bare line of events.

    logline vs. synopsis: A logline goes further than a synopsis: a synopsis walks through the plot in a page, but a logline crushes the entire story into one selling sentence.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    neutral, practical

    Synopsis is the standard word wherever a story must be known before it is experienced: agents request a synopsis of an unpublished manuscript, programs print one for operas in other languages, and streaming menus run on them. Unlike a teaser or blurb, a synopsis is allowed — often expected — to give the ending away; its job is orientation, not temptation. The plural is synopses.

    The community theater asked Jonah, its youngest volunteer, to write the program notes for the fall play. His first draft ran three pages and quoted his favorite lines. The director handed it back with one instruction: give them a synopsis, not an essay. So Jonah started over and walked the plot from beginning to end in nine compact lines — the shipwreck, the disguise, the twin mistaken for her brother, the double wedding. He left out his opinions, the staging notes, and every quotation. On opening night he watched a grandmother read the program to her grandson, and by the dimming of the lights the boy could follow every scene.
