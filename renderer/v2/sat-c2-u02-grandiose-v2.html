Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Grandiose means grand in an inflated way that outruns real substance, sense, or means.

See the word in context

The garage band's plans were grandiose from the start: a world tour, a triple album, and matching gold jackets — all before their first paying gig.

The freshman film club voted for the grandiose option: a three-hour space epic with battle fleets, invented alien languages, and a full orchestra — to be shot on one borrowed camera over two weekends with a budget of $180.

Grandiose is not just 'grand': the -iose ending curdles the grandeur, signaling scale that has outrun sense — plans, buildings, or talk too big for their means, impressive on paper and impractical in fact.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    overblown: Overblown faults the telling: a review, a claim, or a reputation inflated past what the substance underneath supports. Grandiose faults the thing itself — plans, buildings, and promises whose scale has outrun their means. An overblown report of a modest success exaggerates; a grandiose plan was oversized from the start.

    puffed-up: Puffed-up describes swelling past true size — an ego, a price, a title — and the swelling can be small and private. Grandiose needs real scale: schemes or structures pretending to a grandeur their means cannot honor. A puffed-up clerk brags; a grandiose one drafts plans for a marble headquarters.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    realistic: scaled to what can actually be done

    understated: kept purposely smaller and quieter than it could be

    Where it sits · how far the claim exceeds reality

    showy → grandiose → delusional

    showy vs. grandiose: Showy only wants attention — sequins and fireworks — while grandiose makes a claim about scale: the thing pretends to a grandeur its means cannot honor.

    delusional vs. grandiose: Delusional lies past grandiose: a grandiose plan is impractical but still recognizably a plan, while a delusional one has broken with reality altogether.

      Section 3

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      critical / judging

      Grandiose is a judgment, not a compliment: use it when scale has outrun sense — grandiose schemes, grandiose promises. Genuine majesty deserves grand or magnificent; and note the difference from this unit's sweeping, which describes wide reach without sneering at it. Calling a plan grandiose says you doubt it will survive contact with a budget.

      In 1893, land promoter Elias Crane announced that the prairie town of Dry Creek -- population 312 -- would become 'the Chicago of the West.' His plans were grandiose even by the standards of a boom decade: a twelve-story opera house, a stock exchange, and a canal connecting the town to a river forty miles away. Crane printed maps showing steamboats docking where, in fact, there was only buffalo grass. The town's entire treasury could not have paid for one block of the opera house. A few settlers bought lots. Most read the numbers, looked at the dry creek bed, and kept their savings. Work on the canal stopped after a hundred yards; the trench is still visible today, a scar recording the distance between Crane's vision and his means.
