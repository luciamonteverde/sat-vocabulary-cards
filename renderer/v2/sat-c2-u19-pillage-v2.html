Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Pillage means to rob a place violently, especially in war.

See the word in context

Norse raiders pillaged the island abbey in a single tide, carrying off the plate, the relics, and the winter grain.

In August of 410, Alaric's Goths spent three days pillaging Rome. They worked with terrifying speed and order: gold from the temples, silver from the great houses, grain wagons from the riverside warehouses — but they burned little and battered less, and on the fourth day they marched out with the city still standing behind them.

Pillage is the exact word for those three days, because it means to rob a place violently, especially in war — and robbery is the whole of it. Pillaging armies take; what they leave is emptied, not leveled. Loot is its street-scale cousin; ransack is the rough search of one house. Pillage is theft at the scale of towns — and its unit-mate raze is what happens when the army wants nothing left at all.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    ransack: To ransack is to tear one place apart in a rough, violent search — a house, a shop, a single desk — hunting for whatever is worth taking. Pillage is bigger and blunter: an army stripping a whole town of its goods by force, no searching required.

    plunder: To plunder is to strip a place of its valuables by open force — the victor's haul, the raider's right — and the word can even name the goods themselves. Pillage is its war-zone twin: always the violent act of robbing a town, never the goods.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    safeguard: to guard it from harm

    defend: to stand between it and the attackers

    Where it sits · the scale and force of the theft

    swipe → loot → pillage

    swipe vs. pillage: A swipe is theft at its smallest — one item, palmed on the sly, with no force at all — where pillage is theft at the scale of a town, taken by open force.

    loot vs. pillage: Loot goes further than swipe — armfuls carried out of the disorder — but pillage claims the most: a place stripped wholesale, by force, down to its stores.

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    historical / martial

    Pillage lives in war reporting and history: armies pillage, and what they take is the point. Loot fits civil disorder; ransack, a single violent search. Keep the unit's line bright — pillage is theft, raze is demolition. A pillaged town is poorer; a razed one is gone.
