Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Ambivalent means having opposing feelings.

See the word in context

Keiko is ambivalent about her twin sister moving in next door, treasuring the company and mourning her quiet mornings in equal measure.

When the buyout offer arrived, the workshop's founder felt fiercely ambivalent: the price honored thirty years of craft, and accepting it would end them.

Ambivalent is more exact than 'unsure' because it doesn't mean lacking an opinion — it means holding two strong, opposing feelings at once, each pulling with real force.

    Accuracy

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    mixed: Mixed feelings contain both good and bad reactions, in any proportion — a mild, catch-all report that says nothing about strength. Ambivalent is more exact: two opposing feelings, each strong, held at the same time and pulling with real force.

    conflicted: Conflicted stages a fight between loyalties or duties — serving one side feels like betraying the other. Ambivalent is the wider, cooler word: it reports two opposing feelings held together, with real force on both sides, but without insisting the two are at war.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    wholehearted: feeling one way completely, with nothing held back

    decided: settled in your view, with the wavering over

    Where it sits · how strongly both sides pull

    unsure → ambivalent → torn

    unsure vs. ambivalent: Unsure is milder than ambivalent — it means you haven't formed a feeling yet, not that two strong feelings are pulling against each other.

    torn vs. ambivalent: Torn goes further than ambivalent: the two feelings stop coexisting and start ripping, so that choosing either way feels like a loss.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    neutral / formal

    Use ambivalent when someone holds two opposing feelings at once — not when they simply haven't decided. Its unit-mate conflicted adds a clash of loyalties or duties; ambivalent is the wider, cooler word for feeling both ways.

    The diary of Nell Prentice, kept through 1871, records the year the railroad reached her Kansas town. Historians quote her because she refuses to simplify. The line brought her brother's letters in four days instead of four weeks, and she blesses it for that on one page. On the next she mourns what the whistle ended: the long quiet evenings, the pace of a town that had never needed a timetable. She calls herself ambivalent, and means it exactly — 'gratitude and grief, both of them all the way,' she writes, refusing to let either feeling shave the other down. The diary never resolves. The last entry still holds the two feelings side by side, at full strength, without apology.
