What It Means

Umbrage means offense or resentment at a slight.

See the word in context

She took umbrage at being seated by the kitchen doors and spent the whole dinner radiating polite frost.

The sommelier took umbrage when the table ordered cola with the tasting menu; he served it, bowed, and did not come back.

Umbrage is offense at a slight — smaller than outrage, more wounded than annoyance, and usually worn where others can see it. It almost always travels in one phrase: you take umbrage at something, the way you take a hit.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    offense: Offense is the everyday, all-purpose word for feeling insulted, and it says nothing about size or duration. Umbrage is offense in formal dress: taken at a slight, worn where others can see it, and nursed a while. Anyone gives offense; umbrage must be taken.

    resentment: Resentment is a longer-simmering sense of unfair treatment — it can build for months over pay, chores, or credit. Umbrage arrives all at once, at one identifiable slight, and is usually visible on the spot. Resentment accumulates quietly; umbrage is taken, publicly, at something.

    indignation: Indignation is offense with a righteous edge — anger that a rule of fairness has been broken, often on someone else's behalf. Umbrage is more personal and smaller: wounded dignity at a slight to oneself. The indignant cite a principle; those taking umbrage cite the insult to themselves.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    amusement: taking the remark as a joke instead

    contentment: quiet satisfaction that nothing has ruffled

    Where it sits · how far the hurt feeling swells

    pique → umbrage → outrage

    pique vs. umbrage: Pique is a flash of wounded pride, gone by dessert — lighter than umbrage, which settles in and stays the evening.

    outrage vs. umbrage: Outrage goes further than umbrage: a public roar of anger, where umbrage smolders at a personal slight.

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    formal / wry

    Umbrage almost always appears in the phrase take umbrage at, and it suits wounded dignity better than real injury. Pique is briefer; outrage is bigger and more public. Beside wrath and rancor in this unit, umbrage is the pocket-sized grievance — but the most quotable.

    For forty years Harold Pruitt's hardware store had anchored the Founders' Day parade, his flatbed of antique tools rolling directly behind the mayor. This year the printed lineup put him ninth, behind a smoothie franchise. Harold read the sheet twice at the counter, folded it, and took umbrage in full view of six customers. He said nothing rude. He simply wore the insult like a boutonniere: a stiff nod for the parade chairman, regrets sent to the pancake breakfast, his famous window display gone dark for the week. It was not the town he was angry with, and no great principle was at stake. It was one slight, to him, and everyone was allowed to see exactly what he thought of it. Forty years of goodwill, the barber observed, and one line of type had undone it — for exactly as long as Harold chose to be seen minding.
