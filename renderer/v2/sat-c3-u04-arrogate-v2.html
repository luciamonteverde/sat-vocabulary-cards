arrogate

/AIR-uh-gayt/ - the stress falls on AIR

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Arrogate means to seize a power or right without any authority to do so.

See the word in context

The referee arrogated the league's power to suspend players, announcing mid-match bans that only the disciplinary panel may impose.

During the blackout, the building's head janitor arrogated emergency powers no lease had ever granted him — locking the roof, rationing elevator keys, fining 'violators' — and residents obeyed for two days before anyone thought to check the rules.

Arrogate is sharper than 'seize' or 'grab' because it is specifically about powers and rights, not things, and it always implies the taker had no authority whatsoever — the same Latin family that gives us arrogant, someone who claims more than they are owed.

    Accuracy

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    commandeer: To commandeer is to seize a physical thing — a vehicle or a building — for immediate use, often in an emergency. Arrogate never takes objects: it seizes powers and rights, always without authority. You commandeer a truck during a flood; you arrogate the right to give the orders.

    expropriate: To expropriate is to take property, usually a government acting with some legal cover — an order signed, compensation set. Arrogate has no cover at all: it means seizing a power or right with no authority whatsoever, and the word carries that accusation built in.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    renounce: to give up a claim or right formally

    waive: to choose not to use a right you actually hold

    Where it sits · how far beyond rightful authority it goes

    claim → assume → arrogate

    claim vs. arrogate: Claiming a right is much milder than arrogating it — a claim merely states that the right should be yours, and leaves everyone free to argue.

    assume vs. arrogate: Assuming a power is weaker than arrogating it — you can assume duties legitimately when a role falls to you, while arrogating means snatching them with no authority at all.

      Section 3

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      formal / legal

      Arrogate belongs to constitutions, bylaws, and board minutes — wherever powers are written down and someone helps themselves to one anyway. For everyday grabbing, 'take' or 'seize' will do; arrogate adds the accusation that the taker had no right, with a whiff of arrogance built into the word. Its near-twin usurp — a word you will meet in a later unit — leans more toward stealing a position or throne, while arrogate targets powers and rights.

Yet in the quiet of August, the acting director sold two, and by September she had begun to arrogate the board's whole authority over the collection, approving loans and trades on her signature alone. When trustees objected, she produced no rule, no vote, no delegation -- only the argument that someone had to act while the board was slow. The distinction mattered at the hearing: an official who oversteps with permission has made an error; one who seizes a power never granted has done something else. The board's counsel used the old word for it deliberately, and no one in the room mistook it for a compliment.
