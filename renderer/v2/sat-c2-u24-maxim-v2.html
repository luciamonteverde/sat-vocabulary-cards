Meaning

Hear It

What It Means

Maxim means a short saying expressing a rule of conduct.

See the word in context

Her tennis coach owns exactly one maxim — 'Play the point, not the score' — and applies it to everything from serves to algebra.

The pilot's kneeboard carries a maxim in grease pencil, older than the airline itself: 'Aviate, navigate, communicate — in that order.' Every simulator emergency, the instructors say, is finally a test of whether you obey those five words when the cockpit starts shouting.

Maxim is the right term because it is a short saying expressing a rule of conduct — not a fact, not a joke, but marching orders compressed to pocket size. A proverb carries folk wisdom with no author; a maxim reads like law you could act on this minute.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    proverb: A proverb is folk wisdom with the author worn away — everybody's saying, describing how life tends to go. A maxim reads like marching orders instead: a rule of conduct you could act on this minute. A proverb observes the world; a maxim tells you what to do in it.

    adage: An adage is an old saying grown respectable simply by surviving — age is its credential, and it may only observe. A maxim earns its keep by usefulness instead: however young, it compresses a rule of conduct into a sentence you can act on.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    rant: heat and length with no distilled lesson

    nonsense: words offering nothing to live by

    Where it sits · how much lasting guidance the saying carries

    catchphrase → slogan → maxim

    catchphrase vs. maxim: A catchphrase is sound with stickiness and nothing more — fun to repeat, empty of instruction, the far end of the line from a maxim's usable rule.

    slogan vs. maxim: A slogan stops short of a maxim — it rallies you to a brand or a cause for a season, while a maxim hands you a rule of conduct that outlives its coiner.

      The head chef at Marrow keeps a single maxim taped inside every station: 'Taste before you send.' It is not decoration. New cooks learn fast that the four words are a working rule, applied a hundred times a night, from the sauce pot to the pastry bench. When a reviewer once asked where the line came from, the chef shrugged and said she had made it up during a disastrous first year. That answer surprises people who expect kitchen wisdom to be old, handed down through generations of chefs. But the line earns its place the only way a maxim can — by being usable, every single shift, by anyone holding a spoon.
