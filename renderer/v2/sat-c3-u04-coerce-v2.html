Meaning

Hear It

What It Means

Coerce means to force someone to act through threats or pressure.

See the word in context

The loan shark coerced shop owners into paying 'protection' fees by hinting at what happened to stores that refused.

The team captain coerced the younger players into 'volunteering' for his extra laps — anyone who said no found his gear missing on match day.

Coerce is stronger than 'pressure' and different from 'persuade': persuasion wins a free yes, while coercion manufactures a yes through fear. The moment agreement is given freely, it is no longer coercion.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    compel: To compel is to force by any means, including bare circumstance — an empty fuel tank can compel a change of plans, with no one threatening anyone. Coerce always needs a person applying the pressure: the yes is manufactured through threats or fear, which is why the word accuses.

    browbeat: To browbeat is to bully someone face to face — words, glares, and repetition until they give in. Coerce is broader and darker: the pressure runs through threats of real harm, not just an overbearing manner, and it can be applied from a distance.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    persuade: to win agreement freely, with reasons

    coax: to tempt someone gently and patiently toward a choice

    Where it sits · how little freedom to refuse remains

    nudge → arm-twisting → coerce

    nudge vs. coerce: A nudge is far gentler than coercing — a hint, a reminder, a little push that leaves the choice entirely free.

    arm-twisting vs. coerce: Arm-twisting is milder than coercing — heavy, insistent pressure, but still short of the open threats that make refusal feel dangerous.

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      formal / legal

      Coerce is the word courts and reporters use when a yes was manufactured by fear — it always implies wrongdoing. 'Pressure' is the everyday cousin; 'force' covers physical compulsion too. Coerce names the act; its unit-mate duress names the unlawful pressure the victim was under — you coerce someone, who then acts under duress.
