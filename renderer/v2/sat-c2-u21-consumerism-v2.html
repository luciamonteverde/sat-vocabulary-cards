Meaning

Hear It

What It Means

Consumerism means a culture that prizes the constant buying of goods.

See the word in context

Black Friday footage of tents outside the mall is the news channel's yearly shorthand for consumerism.

In Grandpa's shed hangs one hammer, its handle replaced twice, its head older than the house. In the garage next door sit four cordless drills, three of them gifts, one still sealed in its box, and a calendar of sales taped to the wall so no doorbuster slips past. Two households, two philosophies — thrift in the shed, consumerism in the garage — and only one of them needs the calendar.

Consumerism is the exact word for the garage's philosophy, because consumerism means a culture that prizes the constant buying of goods — the point is not the drills but the buying of drills, a rhythm of acquiring that no toolbox can ever finish. Greed is one person's appetite; consumerism is a whole culture cheering the appetite on.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    materialism: Materialism prizes the owning: it measures people and happiness by possessions, and no shopping needs to happen at all. Consumerism prizes the endless buying — the pleasure lives in acquiring, not holding. A materialist could sit content on a full vault; consumerism needs the registers ringing.

    acquisitiveness: Acquisitiveness is the raw appetite for getting more, and it lives inside one person. Consumerism is that appetite scaled up into a whole culture — sales calendars, doorbusters, a society cheering the buying on. One greedy collector shows acquisitiveness; a whole society of buyers shows consumerism.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    thrift: spend little, keep it long

    minimalism: own little, on purpose

    Where it sits · how far acquiring things takes over life

    splurging → consumerism → hoarding

    splurging vs. consumerism: Splurging is far smaller than consumerism — one indulgent afternoon of treats and life goes back to normal, while consumerism keeps a whole culture buying all year round.

    hoarding vs. consumerism: Hoarding goes further than consumerism — the consumerist keeps buying for the pleasure of new things; the hoarder's possessions pile up past use, past room, past sense.

      Word Choice

      critical / journalistic

      Consumerism names the culture, usually with an edge of criticism. Its economics cousins in this unit divide the map: consumerism is households buying, corporatism is organized industries steering the state, mercantilism is the state steering trade to hoard gold, and laissez-faire is the state declining to steer at all. Materialism, meanwhile, judges hearts rather than economies.

      Our economics unit ended with a study of the 1890s mail-order catalog, which the textbook credits with carrying consumerism into homes a hundred miles from any store. Before the catalog, a farm family bought what wore out: a plow blade, boots, salt. The catalog taught a different rhythm — six hundred pages arriving twice a year, page after page of things nobody had known to want. Diaries from the period show families reading it aloud in the evenings like a serial novel, circling items they would never need but fully intended to order. The historian's dry summary stuck with me: the catalog's real product was wanting.

        The sociologist who studied the yacht-club suburb opens her book with its central puzzle: the district's ____ is total, yet almost nothing is ever bought there. Families keep the same cars for decades; the shops near the marina struggle. Worth is reckoned entirely in holdings — the vintage of a sailboat, the acreage behind a hedge — and a family's standing rises or falls with an appraisal, never with a purchase. The appetite, she writes, is not for getting more but for being seen to have.
