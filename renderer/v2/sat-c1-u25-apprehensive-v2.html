apprehensive

/ap-rih-HEN-siv/

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Apprehensive means anxious or uneasy that something bad may be coming — gripped in advance by dread of a specific event ahead.

See the word in context

She grew apprehensive as the interview panel fell silent and slowly passed her portfolio down the table.

The morning of her first solo flight, Dana ran the preflight checklist twice, then a third time. The instructor had signed her off; the weather was perfect; nothing was wrong. Still, taxiing to the runway alone, she caught herself rehearsing engine-failure procedures for a plane that hummed flawlessly, her mind seizing on every way the next forty minutes could go wrong. She was not in danger — she was apprehensive.

Nothing bad has happened, and nothing may: Dana's fear is aimed entirely at what lies ahead, her thoughts gripped in advance by imagined trouble. That forward-looking dread of a specific coming event is exactly what apprehensive describes.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    anxious: Anxious means troubled by worry, and the worry can be broad or free-floating — you can feel anxious about everything and nothing. Apprehensive points its dread at a target: an apprehensive person is uneasy about a specific event coming toward them — a test, a verdict, a flight.

    wary: Wary means alert to possible danger and keeping your guard up — wariness lives in watchful behavior, in the double-check and the careful step. Apprehensive names the inner dread itself: you can act wary while feeling calm, but you cannot be apprehensive without the knot of fear about what may come.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    confident: sure that things will turn out well

    unfazed: not troubled by what may be coming

    Where it sits · how strongly fear of what may come takes hold

    uneasy → apprehensive → panicked

    uneasy vs. apprehensive: Uneasy is weaker than apprehensive: an uneasy feeling is a vague flicker of discomfort that may have no clear cause, while apprehension grips harder — a named dread fixed on something specific ahead.

    panicked vs. apprehensive: Panicked is stronger than apprehensive: an apprehensive person still thinks and functions while the dread builds, but panic overwhelms thought entirely, scattering it just when it is needed most.

      Section 3

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      neutral; common in narrative, journalism, and academic prose

      Apprehensive usually takes 'about' or 'that' ('apprehensive about the merger'). Keep it apart from this unit's restive, which is the fidgety impatience of being held back, not fear of what's ahead, and from pensive, which is quiet, absorbed thought that may carry no fear at all. And note the trap inside its own family: apprehend now usually means to arrest, but apprehensive keeps the older mental grasp — the mind closing around a feared tomorrow.
