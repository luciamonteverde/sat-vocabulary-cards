Meaning

What It Means

Rancor means deep, long-lasting bitterness or ill will.

See the word in context

Some rancor still seasons every casserole Aunt May brings to the reunion — forty years since the will was read, and Cousin Ed's portion is still the smallest.

The treaty ended the border war but not the rancor: veterans on both sides taught their grandchildren the insults first and the anthem second.

Rancor is bitterness with a long shelf life — deeper than a grudge, older than anger, and patient. Where fury burns out in an afternoon, rancor keeps: ill will that has soaked into the wood and stays there for years.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    bitterness: Bitterness is the everyday word for soured feeling, at any depth and any duration — an afternoon's disappointment or a decade's feud. Rancor is bitterness at the far end of that range: deep, old, and durable, ill will that has soaked in and stays for years.

    acrimony: Acrimony is bitterness breaking out into the open — sharp words, cutting exchanges, a divorce fought line by line. Rancor does not need to speak: it can sit in silence for years and still poison a room. Acrimony is heard; rancor is kept.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    warmth: open, affectionate goodwill

    forgiveness: the release of a wrong, letting the debt go

    Where it sits · how stubbornly the resentment stays

    annoyance → grudge → rancor

    annoyance vs. rancor: Annoyance fades by dinner — nothing like rancor, which can outlive the quarrel that started it.

    grudge vs. rancor: A grudge stores one grievance on one shelf; it stops short of rancor, bitterness spread deep and kept for years.

    The two survivors of the Endeavour ice party wrote memoirs forty years apart, and neither ever printed the other's name. Readers assumed the silence was grief. The families knew it was rancor: each man believed, to his last day, that the other had eaten more than his share in the dark months. They never argued publicly. They simply arranged their lives so as never to meet — different clubs, different harbors, different funerals. One grandson found a lecture poster from 1934 with a single name crossed out in pen, pressed flat in a book like a specimen. The feud outlived them both; the two families still sit apart at reunions of the expedition society.
