What It Means

Carouse means to party, celebrate.

See the word in context

When exams ended, the med students caroused through the old quarter until the cafes opened for breakfast.

When the show finally closed after nine hundred performances, the cast caroused backstage till dawn — costume racks for dance partners, prop crowns on every head, the stage manager leading toasts from a ladder.

Carouse is celebration at full volume, and never alone: carousers come in groups, usually loud, usually singing, traditionally with glasses in hand. You can celebrate quietly at your desk; you cannot quietly carouse.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    party: Party is the plain modern verb for celebrating in company — any volume, any hour, cake or karaoke. Carouse is party's rowdy, old-fashioned uncle: always loud, always in a group, traditionally with glasses raised and songs half-remembered. Every carousal is a party; most parties never get near carousing.

    roister: Roister is carouse's near-twin from the same old taverns: to celebrate noisily and swagger about while you do it. The shade of difference is the strut — roisterers show off, spoiling to be noticed, while to carouse is simply to be loud together; the singing matters more than the audience.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    fast: to go without — the empty table to carousing's full one

    mope: to droop through an evening no one would call festive

    Where it sits · how wild the merrymaking gets

    toast → feast → carouse

    toast vs. carouse: A toast is one raised glass and a sentence; carousing is what happens when the toasts stop being counted.

    feast vs. carouse: A feast is grand but still tablebound; carousing is louder and wilder than any feast, spilling past the table into song.

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    informal / a little old-fashioned

    Carouse is the word for loud, festive, usually liquid celebration in company — it smells faintly of taverns and sounds a little antique, which is why it often appears with a wink. For a birthday dinner, celebrate is neutral; and note the split inside this unit: carousing is rowdy at the table, cavorting is rowdy in motion.

    The town archive keeps a folder of complaints from the winter of 1954, the year the curling club finally won the provincial cup. The team, by every account, caroused for three nights straight. They marched from house to house with a borrowed accordion, sang until windows rattled, and emptied the hotel bar of everything but vinegar. 'We were young and the cup was heavy,' one member recalled decades later, entirely unrepentant. What strikes a modern reader is the tone of the complaints themselves: exasperated, but affectionate. The constable's report recommends no charges, noting only that 'the singing was in tune by the third night.' The cup sits in the library now, dented on one side. Nobody remembers how, and the last surviving team member only laughs when asked.
