Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Languid means sluggish from fatigue or weakness.

See the word in context

Jet lag left the whole crew languid, drifting through the soundcheck a half beat behind the drummer.

By the last mile of the charity walk, Grandad's stride had gone languid—each step a small negotiation—so we claimed the shady bench and let the finish line wait.

Languid is more exact than 'tired' because it describes how the tiredness moves: slow, heavy-limbed, drained of push. Sleepy people want a bed; languid people are still going, just at half power.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    listless: Listless is flatness of interest — the listless have energy they can't be bothered to spend, pushing food around the plate. Languid is flatness of strength: heavy limbs, a drifting pace, a body at half power. A listless person could move and doesn't care to; a languid one barely can.

    sluggish: Sluggish describes anything slow to start and slow to move — cold syrup, morning traffic, an old laptop — without saying why. Languid is for living bodies drained by fatigue or weakness, and it can even sound graceful: a languid wave, a languid stroll. Sluggish never flatters; languid sometimes does.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    energetic: charged and ready to move

    lively: quick, bright, and animated

    Where it sits · how much energy is left to spend

    relaxed → languid → exhausted

    relaxed vs. languid: Relaxed is the pleasant, milder state next to languid — muscles loose by choice, energy still on call the moment it's wanted, while languid limbs are heavy because the strength just isn't there.

    exhausted vs. languid: Exhausted goes further than relaxed—an exhausted hiker has nothing left at all, while a languid one still drifts forward at half speed.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    literary / descriptive

    Languid is for weakness you can watch: heavy limbs, drifting pace, a wave that barely lifts. Its unit-mates carve the territory finely—torpid is a deeper shutdown, closer to dormancy; somnolent is specifically sleepy; and listless (a chip above) is flatness of interest rather than of strength. If nothing is wrong and the slowness is delicious, 'lazy afternoon' may serve better than languid.
