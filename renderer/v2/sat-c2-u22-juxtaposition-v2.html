What It Means

Juxtaposition means the placement of two things side by side to invite comparison.

See the word in context

The florist's window banks on juxtaposition: one desert cactus centered in a sea of ferns, each making the other look stranger.

The centennial exhibit prints the war poem and the recruiting poster on facing pages. Each hung in classrooms alone for a hundred years; set together at last, the poster's shine and the poem's mud argue with each other, and the argument is the juxtaposition's doing.

Juxtaposition names the deliberate placement, not the difference itself. The poem and the poster differed for a century while nobody noticed; only putting them side by side turned the difference into a claim. Contrast is what you see; juxtaposition is what the arranger did.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    side-by-side pairing: A side-by-side pairing is the plain physical fact: two things set next to each other, for any reason or none. A juxtaposition adds intent — someone arranged the pair so that each would comment on the other. Every juxtaposition is a side-by-side pairing, but only the deliberate, point-making ones earn the longer word.

    counterpoint: A counterpoint is a pairing in which each element answers the other — the second thing exists to talk back to the first, the way one melody answers another. Juxtaposition is quieter about roles: it names the deliberate placement itself, whether or not the two pieces answer each other at all.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    separation: keeping things apart so neither comments on the other

    blending: merging elements until no seam remains

    Where it sits · how pointed the contrast between them grows

    grouping → juxtaposition → clash

    grouping vs. juxtaposition: A grouping is milder than a juxtaposition — things gathered for convenience, with no comparison intended.

    clash vs. juxtaposition: A clash goes further than a juxtaposition: juxtaposition sets two things beside each other to talk, while a clash has them at war.

      Word Choice

      academic / arts

      Juxtaposition is the arranger's word: it credits a choice, not an accident. If you mean the difference itself, say contrast; if two things merely happen to sit together, adjacency will do. Say juxtaposition when someone placed them side by side to make a point.

      The film editor Dana Reyes says her favorite tool costs nothing: order. In her documentary about a shrinking prairie town, she cuts from the packed 1954 homecoming parade straight to the same street today, empty at noon. Neither shot is remarkable alone. The parade footage is grainy; the modern street is flat and gray. A festival board once asked her to drop the sequence entirely. Set together, though, each image makes the other speak: the crowd measures the emptiness, and the emptiness gives the crowd its weight. She calls the cut a juxtaposition, and she chooses the word carefully — the placement is an argument, not an accident. Reyes never adds narration to these moments. The viewer's own comparison, she says, is louder than anything she could write.
