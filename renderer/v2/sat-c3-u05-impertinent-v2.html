impertinent

/im-PUR-tuh-nuhnt/ - the stress falls on PUR

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Impertinent means rude and disrespectful, especially toward someone deserving courtesy.

See the word in context

The new page thought it clever to ask the visiting ambassador why her speech had run long; the embassy staff found the question impertinent.

Midway through the retired concertmaster's masterclass, an impertinent first-year violinist announced that the old man's bowing had gone out of fashion. The studio went very quiet.

Impertinent is not the word for mere bluntness or curiosity — it marks the overstep: a remark that disrespects someone owed courtesy, from a junior toward a senior, a guest toward a host, a stranger toward anyone. The im- is the true negative in-: 'not pertaining' — none of your business, said anyway.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    prying: Prying means digging into private matters — reading over shoulders, pressing for details nobody offered. It is one common route to being impertinent, but prying targets secrets, while impertinent marks the disrespect itself: an overstepping remark aimed at someone owed courtesy.

    intrusive: Intrusive describes anything pushing in where it is not wanted — questions, noise, even advertising — and it passes no verdict about respect. Impertinent adds exactly that verdict: the intrusion insults someone owed courtesy, the junior prying at the senior, the guest at the host.

    forward: Forward means bolder and more familiar than the situation allows — first names too soon, questions too personal — and it can still be friendly. An impertinent remark goes further: the boldness reads as plain disrespect toward someone whose position deserved courtesy.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    courteous: showing the politeness the moment calls for

    respectful: giving rank, age, or feeling its proper weight

    Where it sits · how much disrespect the prying shows

    curious → nosy → impertinent

    curious vs. impertinent: Curious is innocent — simply wanting to know — and stops well short of impertinent, which turns the wanting into an offense.

    nosy vs. impertinent: Nosy pokes into other people's business but still stops short of impertinent, which adds open disrespect toward someone owed courtesy.

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    formal

    Impertinent stresses the overstep — the junior questioning the senior, the guest prying at the table. Its neighbor impudent (also in this unit) is bolder still and utterly unembarrassed, and irreverence aims at solemn things rather than persons. In everyday talk, rude or out of line does the job.

    In 1893 a junior clerk at a London shipping firm wrote directly to the Duchess of Weare, a major shareholder, to say her latest instructions would sink the company. The letter was flawlessly polite in its wording -- and, by every rule of the age, shockingly impertinent. The offense was not the argument, which turned out to be correct, nor any secret pried loose; it was the act itself: a clerk of three months presuming to correct a duchess. His manager apologized on the firm's behalf and quietly filed the letter away. Forty years later, the same letter hung framed in the boardroom, its judgment vindicated -- though etiquette manuals of the day would still have called it an outrage.
