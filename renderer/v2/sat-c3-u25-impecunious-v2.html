Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

What It Means

Impecunious means having little or no money.

See the word in context

Impecunious again after the van repairs, the band split one milkshake four ways to toast the tour.

The impecunious inventor heated her workshop by running the prototype, drafted patents on the backs of overdue notices, and bartered garden vegetables for an hour of machine-shop time each week.

Impecunious is more precise than 'poor' because it points squarely at the wallet—a chronic shortage of money—without implying misery in every other respect; an impecunious student can be perfectly happy.

    Accuracy

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    broke: Broke is the plain, informal word for having no money right now — something you say about yourself between paychecks. Impecunious is its dressed-up, gently comic cousin, and it stretches the condition out: the impecunious are chronically short of money, not just caught at a bad moment.

    impoverished: Impoverished means made poor — it hints at a downfall, and often at deprivation reaching beyond money into whole living conditions. Impecunious is narrower and lighter: it points at the wallet alone, a lasting shortage of cash that implies no misery in the rest of life.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    wealthy: holding money and property in abundance

    flush: informally, well supplied with cash for the moment

    Where it sits · how deep and lasting the money shortage

    strapped → impecunious → penniless

    strapped vs. impecunious: Strapped stops short of impecunious — strapped means briefly short of cash, not chronically without it.

    penniless vs. impecunious: Penniless goes further than impecunious — penniless means the money is entirely gone, down to the last coin.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    formal / often wry

    Impecunious is the dressed-up, often gently comic word for being short of money — biographies love an 'impecunious young poet.' In conversation, 'broke' is plainer. Keep it apart from its unit-mate penurious: the impecunious have nothing to spend, while the penurious have money and refuse to spend it.

    Every block has one, my grandmother said: ours was Mr. Okafor, who could repair anything and afford almost nothing. He had been that way for thirty years, she told me — not ruined, never desperate, just permanently and cheerfully short of money. His porch light ran off a bicycle wheel. His coat was older than most of the cars on the street. When the bakery paid him for fixing its mixer, he spent the fee that afternoon on parts for a neighbor's radio. The word my grandmother used for him was impecunious, and she said it the way you would say 'incorrigible' about someone you loved. It never meant he was suffering, she insisted. It meant that money, alone among the things that passed through his hands, refused to stay.
