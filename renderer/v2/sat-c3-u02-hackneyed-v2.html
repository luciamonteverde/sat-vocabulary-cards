Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Hackneyed means so overused that it has lost all freshness; trite.

See the word in context

The wedding planner kept a blacklist of hackneyed toasts and fined any best man who opened with a dictionary definition of love.

The slogan contest for the new sports drink drew four hundred entries, and the judges binned three hundred as hackneyed in a single afternoon: 'Just add thirst.' 'Unleash the beast.' 'No limits.' Each had sounded bold once, decades ago, and had been printed on so many bottles since that they no longer registered as words at all.

Hackneyed is sharper than 'stale' because it names the cause: something so overused that it has lost all freshness. A phrase can go stale quietly, from age alone; a hackneyed one has been worked to death in public, and everyone can hear the thousand mouths that used it first.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    banal: Banal means dull because it was never interesting to begin with — a banal remark about the weather is flat on its very first use. Hackneyed blames a history instead: the phrase was striking once, and a thousand reuses wore the freshness away. Banal never had spark; hackneyed had it and lost it to overuse.

    threadbare: Threadbare means worn thin from constant handling — a coat, an excuse, an alibi produced so often it barely covers anything now. Hackneyed is specifically about freshness in language and ideas: overused by so many mouths that nothing striking is left. A threadbare excuse still limps along; a hackneyed phrase has been emptied out.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    fresh: newly made, not yet dulled by use

    original: the first of its kind, owing nothing to models

    Where it sits · how worn down by repetition it is

    common → stale → hackneyed

    common vs. hackneyed: Common only counts how often something appears; a common phrase can still do honest work, far from hackneyed.

    stale vs. hackneyed: Stale goes further than common — the freshness has gone flat — but hackneyed is the end of the road, worn featureless by a thousand thoughtless reuses.

      Section 3

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      critical / literary

      Hackneyed is a critic's word: it does not just call a phrase dull, it names the cause — overuse by a thousand others. Stale says the freshness is gone without saying why; original is its true opposite. Save hackneyed for language and ideas; a worn carpet is just worn.

      Every June the greeting-card writers at Fielding & Sons receive the year's forbidden list from their editor, Miss Okafor. On it go phrases that once landed with real force — 'love conquers all' topped the first list she ever typed. Her rule is simple: a line goes on the list not for being old but for being hackneyed, spent by ten thousand thoughtless repetitions. A card may quote a century-old poem if the line still startles; it may not open with yesterday's slogan if the slogan has gone numb. New hires grumble until they read the archive and watch a once-bright phrase dim, year by year, into wallpaper.
