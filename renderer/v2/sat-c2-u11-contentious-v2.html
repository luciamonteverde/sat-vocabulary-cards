Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Contentious means having a tendency to quarrel or dispute.

See the word in context

The historical society schedules the retired colonel last on every program, since the contentious old man will dispute whatever talk precedes his.

Every book club has one. The contentious Marguerite arrives with flags in her paperback and challenges ready: the ending was unearned, the hero was not to be trusted, and whatever anyone admires she will gladly contest until the coffee runs out. Nobody escapes: she has disputed the club's snack rotation, its flyer font, and, memorably, the dictionary itself.

Contentious is the exact word because it names a tendency to quarrel or dispute — a standing habit, not a bad night. Marguerite isn't angry and holds no grudges; she simply cannot let a claim go by unquestioned. That itch to contest, wherever it lands, is what contentious describes.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    argumentative: An argumentative person loves the back-and-forth of arguing and will gladly take either side just to keep a debate alive. Contentious is sharper: a contentious person goes hunting for claims to contest and turns ordinary discussion into dispute. Argumentative enjoys the exchange; contentious picks the quarrel and presses it.

    disputatious: Disputatious is the bookish twin — given to disputing everything, most at home in seminar rooms and learned journals. A contentious person has the same standing tendency to quarrel, but the word is everyday English and covers whole families, panels, and issues. When the disputing wears a tweed jacket, it is disputatious; at the dinner table, contentious.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    agreeable: pleasant and quick to go along

    easygoing: hard to draw into any dispute at all

    Where it sits · how readily views turn into fights

    opinionated → contentious → belligerent

    opinionated vs. contentious: An opinionated person is full of firm views but may keep them holstered; a contentious one turns views into disputes.

    belligerent vs. contentious: Belligerent goes past contentious — from quarreling over points to spoiling for the fight itself.

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      neutral / slightly formal

      Contentious is for people (and panels, and families) with a standing tendency to dispute. Its cluster-mates in this unit split the ground: pugnacious wants the fight itself, and fractious is irritable and hard to manage without needing a point to argue. Contentious quarrels have content — a claim to contest. (You'll also meet a contentious issue — one that makes people dispute; the habit is the same, worn by a topic.)

      The community orchestra seats ninety musicians, and eighty-nine of them get along. The exception is Herr Brandt, second oboe, the most contentious man ever to hold a reed. He hunts the rehearsal schedule for things to fight about: the tuning note is too short, the bowing is historically wrong, the intermission favors the brass. It is never one complaint and done; he presses each point through the break and into the parking lot. The conductor has learned to hand him a claim worth contesting early -- a tempo marking, say -- the way one throws a stick for a dog.
