Meaning

Hear It

What It Means

Espionage means the practice of using secret agents to steal a rival's guarded information — spying carried on as an organized trade, between governments or between companies.

See the word in context

The perfume house stored its unreleased formulas in a windowless basement vault, a precaution aimed less at fire than at espionage.

For years the harbor's most patient 'birdwatcher' aimed her binoculars just past the gulls, noting which warships took on fuel and how deep they sat in the water, and mailed the counts abroad folded inside knitting patterns. When counterintelligence officers finally searched her cottage, they found the whole machinery of espionage shelved between seed catalogs: a codebook, a schedule of drop points, and pay slips from a foreign ministry.

Espionage names the organized practice, not a single stolen glance: an agent working in a rival's service, a cover story, and a channel for moving guarded information out. That professional machinery is what separates it from ordinary prying — a neighbor who peers over the fence is snooping; the 'birdwatcher,' salaried and coded, was committing espionage.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    surveillance: Surveillance is watching: a camera in a lobby, a detective parked outside a door — steady observation of what someone does, often lawful and sometimes openly announced. Espionage goes further than watching: it uses secret agents to take information a rival is deliberately guarding.

    reconnaissance: Reconnaissance is the scout's survey — a look at ground, defenses, or positions taken from the outside, usually just ahead of an operation. Espionage works from the inside and over time: an agent in someone's service, taking what no ridge-top view could ever show.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    transparency: doing business in the open, where anyone can check the facts

    openness: the habit of sharing information freely instead of guarding it

    Where it sits · how organized and grave the secret information-taking is

    snooping → espionage → treason

    snooping vs. espionage: Snooping is milder than espionage: a snoop pries into a coworker's drawer out of idle curiosity, while espionage is an organized, salaried effort to steal a rival's guarded secrets.

    treason vs. espionage: Treason is graver than espionage: espionage can be aimed at any rival, but a citizen who turns that secret trade against his own country commits treason, the heavier crime.

      Section 3

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      formal — the courtroom, newsroom, and history-book word

      Espionage names the practice, not the person or the act: the spy is the agent, an act of espionage is one job, and espionage is the standing trade itself. It is an uncountable noun — no plural, never 'an espionage.' Keep it apart from surveillance, which watches behavior and can be perfectly lawful, and from this unit's surreptitious, which describes the quiet manner of any single act. Between companies, the label is usually industrial or corporate espionage.

      In 1848 the East India Company faced a problem money could not openly solve: China guarded the making of tea as a state secret. So the company hired the botanist Robert Fortune, shaved his head, and sent him into the interior disguised as a merchant from a distant province. For three years Fortune moved from garden to garden, memorizing kiln temperatures, collecting seedlings, and shipping his notes out in stages.
