What It Means

Deleterious means harmful in a subtle or gradual way.

See the word in context

Sleep researchers warn that even dim screen light at midnight can have a deleterious effect on memory that builds unnoticed over weeks.

Jonah kept his earbuds at full volume on every bus ride. His hearing seemed fine week to week, and he laughed off his sister's warnings. Two years later, a school screening showed he had lost part of his high-frequency hearing. The audiologist explained that loud audio has a deleterious effect that accumulates so gradually most listeners never notice it happening.

The harm to Jonah's hearing was real but built up so slowly he never noticed — the subtle, creeping damage that deleterious describes.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    harmful: Harmful is the all-purpose word for anything causing damage or injury — instant or slow, large or small, physical or not. Deleterious is the narrower, more formal term: harm that is subtle or gradual, accumulating before anyone notices. A wasp sting is harmful; a slightly unbalanced diet is deleterious.

    damaging: Damaging means actively doing harm to something, and it fits harm you can point at while it happens — a storm damaging a roof, a leak damaging files. Deleterious harm is quieter: subtle or gradual, easy to miss until it has piled up. Damaging effects announce themselves; deleterious effects creep.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    beneficial: producing good effects

    harmless: causing no damage at all

    Where it sits · how much real harm it does

    unhelpful → deleterious → ruinous

    unhelpful vs. deleterious: Something unhelpful simply fails to do good, while something deleterious actively causes harm.

    ruinous vs. deleterious: Ruinous damage goes far beyond deleterious — it destroys outright rather than harming gradually.

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    formal, academic

    Deleterious appears in research papers and health warnings, usually before words like 'effect' or 'consequences.' Among this unit's harm words, keep the lanes straight: detrimental is the plain, neutral word for anything causing harm; deleterious suggests harm that is subtle or slow to reveal itself; and pernicious is the darkest of the three — harm that spreads insidiously and does deep damage over time.

    When Rome piped water through lead, no one fell ill at the fountain. That was the trouble: lead's effects are deleterious, not dramatic. A sip carried almost nothing. But a city drinks for decades, and the metal that arrived in traces settled quietly into bone. Modern historians argue about how much the pipes mattered, yet the pattern they describe is the one toxicologists know well: no single dose worth noticing, and a burden that only grows. The same signature appears wherever old lead survives — in paint dust, in soil, in solder — harm too slow to alarm anyone on any given day. That slowness is exactly what makes it dangerous. A poison that announced itself would have been ripped out of the walls centuries sooner.
