/BLYT/ - rhymes with night

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Blight means a disease that destroys growing plants.

See the word in context

A blight reached the island's banana groves in June, and by harvest every plantation on the windward slope stood bare.

Wheat rust is a blight older than the alphabet; Roman farmers held a spring festival just to beg it away from their grain. When a new race of the fungus appeared in Uganda in 1999, breeders watched it defeat one resistant variety after another, and seed banks on five continents began crossing wild grasses in a hurry. A single infected stem can shed enough spores to sicken a whole district.

Blight is the exact word for rust, because a blight is a disease that destroys growing plants — and destroys is the operative claim. Mildew films a leaf's surface and spoils its looks; rot takes the tissue it touches and stops there. A blight travels, jumping plant to plant until whole harvests are gone.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    canker: A canker is a localized sore — one wound on one branch or stem that the tree may simply grow around. A blight is never local: it is a disease that races plant to plant and takes whole plantings at once. A canker scars; a blight sweeps.

    infestation: An infestation is an invasion of pests — beetles in the beams, mice in the walls — living things you can trap or spray. A blight is a disease inside the plants themselves, spreading through what it kills until the field is gone. Pests occupy; a blight infects.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    bloom: healthy flowering

    vigor: strong, healthy growth

    Where it sits · how far and fast the damage spreads

    mildew → rot → blight

    mildew vs. blight: Mildew is far below blight — a gray film that dulls the leaf's surface and rarely kills the plant, where blight kills everything it spreads through.

    rot vs. blight: Rot goes further than mildew — the tissue it touches dies — but blight claims the most: it races plant to plant and takes the whole field.

      Apply

      “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

      Word Choice

      plain / agricultural, often figurative

      Blight is literal in the garden and just as famous in the city — planners speak of urban blight eating a neighborhood the way rust eats wheat. Keep it for harm that spreads through what it kills. A bane (this unit) stands outside its victim and wears it down; a blight is inside, moving. Mildew and canker are its smaller cousins.

      In the summer of 1904, a forester at a New York zoo noticed the chestnut trees along the paths dying from the top down. The cause was a fungal blight that had arrived quietly on imported nursery stock, and it moved with terrible speed. Spores rode the wind, the rain, and the feet of birds; a single infected tree could doom a hillside within a few seasons. By 1940 the blight had swept the entire eastern range, and a species that once numbered four billion trees was functionally gone. Foresters could only watch: no spray reached the fungus under the bark, and no chestnut showed real resistance until breeders began crossing rare survivors decades later.
