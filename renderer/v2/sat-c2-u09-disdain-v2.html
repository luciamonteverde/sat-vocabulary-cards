Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Disdain means scorn; the feeling that something is beneath one's respect.

See the word in context

Old Tomas the ferryman regarded outboard motors with quiet disdain, poling his flat-bottomed skiff across the lagoon as his father had.

The chess grandmaster agreed to the exhibition match against a famous streamer, but her disdain was plain from the first move: she played standing up, signed autographs mid-game, and never once glanced at his side of the clock. She didn't hate the man; she had simply ranked him — somewhere below the practice board.

Disdain is the exact word because it names the feeling of looking down: the sense that something sits beneath your respect, too far beneath to deserve anger. Dislike merely turns away; hatred burns energy. Disdain looks down from a height — cool, unbothered, and quietly wounding.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    scorn: Scorn is contempt worn openly — the curled lip, the public jeer, a cold verdict performed for others to see. Disdain is the same downward ranking, but it can stay behind the eyes; scorn, by definition, shows — and neither of them burns.

    contempt: Contempt is the broad, flat feeling that a thing counts for nothing at all. Disdain is more particular: it looks down from a height, ranking a thing beneath one's own standards — which quietly admits the thing still counts for something, just less.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    respect: regard for something's worth

    admiration: warm approval of something done well

    Where it sits · how far the feeling looks down

    dislike → disdain → loathing

    dislike vs. disdain: Dislike just turns away from a thing; disdain climbs a step above it and looks down.

    loathing vs. disdain: Loathing goes further than disdain: what you disdain isn't worth your anger, but what you loathe has earned all of it — hot, personal, and impossible to hide.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    literary / cool

    Disdain names the feeling — the cool, from-above conviction that a thing is beneath your respect. Its unit-mate dismissive names the behavior that feeling produces: the wave, the shrug, the unread report. You can feel disdain and never show it; the moment you flick it at someone, they will call you dismissive.

    Guide Emil Berger has kept the summit log at the Weisshorn hut for thirty years, and he will register any climb. Helicopter-assisted ascents go in the book like all the rest — he is scrupulously fair about dates, names, and weather. But regulars notice a difference in his questions. Walkers are asked about the ridge, the wind, the crux pitch; clients who flew are asked whether they enjoyed the view. Berger has never said a harsh word about the practice, and clients flown to the ridge get the same coffee as everyone else. His disdain lives entirely in that small distinction: no curl of the lip, no complaint in his voice — only a quiet ranking that sets one kind of climb beneath his own standards.
